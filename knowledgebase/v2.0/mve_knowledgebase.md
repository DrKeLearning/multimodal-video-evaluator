# Master Definitions Table

- **Cognitive Load:** The total amount of mental effort being used in the working memory.
- **Seductive Detail:** An interesting but instructionally irrelevant element (like background music with lyrics or a non-functional animation) that distracts from core learning.
- **Social Agency:** The psychological state where a learner perceives the computer/agent as a social partner, increasing their motivation to process information.
- **Salience:** How much a visual element stands out (brightness, motion, size).
- **Relevance:** How important a visual element is to the learning goal.

# Multimedia Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** MM

**Core Definition:** People learn more deeply from words and pictures than from words alone. Adding relevant graphics to verbal messages facilitates the construction of robust mental models.

## Design Guidelines

### Category 1: Fundamental Content Strategy
*Focus: Determining when and why to use graphics.*

### MM-G1. Strategic Graphic-Verbal Integration
- **Priority:** High (Foundational)
- **Category:** Visual Scaffolding / Multimedia Principle
- **Definition:** Combine verbal explanations (text or audio) with relevant graphic/pictorial representations (illustrations, diagrams, animations) rather than using verbal information alone.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Ensure every major verbal concept is anchored by a corresponding graphic element (pictures, illustrations, animations, graphs, charts, diagrams).
- **Rationale:** Humans process information through separate visual and auditory channels. This dual-coding approach creates two distinct knowledge traces—verbal and visual—making retrieval more likely and allowing for the construction of more robust mental models.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Effectiveness decreases if the graphic is purely decorative or irrelevant to the text.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - A fitness video showing an exercise while the instructor explains the proper form.
    - A computerized module on flight principles that includes diagrams to facilitate integrated understanding.
    - A scientific text about hydraulic drum brakes that includes labeled diagrams to illustrate the components.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - A physics textbook describing lightning formation through five paragraphs of text with zero illustrations.
    - A scientific text on brakes that contains only printed words without any visual support.
    - Teaching Newtonian motion principles using a computer-based simulation alone without any accompanying spoken or written instructional text.
    - Presenting a "wall of text" on a slide without a visual anchor, forcing the brain to use only the verbal channel.

### MM-G2. Goal-Orientation: Transfer over Retention
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Strategy
- **Definition:** Design graphics to help learners predict, analyze, or apply knowledge (transfer) rather than just memorizing facts (retention).
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use graphics (illustrations, diagrams, animations), to explain *how* or *why* a system works, not just *what* the parts are. On-screen texts are not considered as graphics.
- **Rationale:** The multimedia principle shows significantly stronger effect sizes for problem-solving and inference generation than for simple recall.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Simply depicting objects and facts mentioned is OK if the narration does not communicate how, why, structure, or process.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - Using a narrated animation to help students build a mental model so they can predict system behavior in a new scenario.
    - Using a combination of animation and narration to facilitate problem-solving in a physics lesson.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - Showing graphics that simply depict objects and facts mentioned through narration but not illustrating the process, system or structure being explained.
    - Talking about how or why but not using graphics/pictures to augment verbal information.

### Category 2: Spatial & Visual Architecture
*Focus: How elements are positioned and connected on the screen.*

### MM-G3. Semantic-Spatial Layouts
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Content Organization
- **Definition:** Use node-link diagrams (concept maps, flowcharts) or grid-based layouts (side-by-side, 2x2 screen division) where spatial proximity and grouping communicates conceptual relationships.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Replace linear bullet points with spatial maps whenever describing relationships or flows.
- **Rationale:** Spatial organization allows learners to see abstract relations (sequence, process, comparison) more clearly than through simple bullet points.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Content difficulty and complexity — this guideline can be ignored when communicating simple, low-difficulty contents as students can easily process them without semantic-spatial layouts.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - A concept map communicating relationships between earthquake faults and plate motion.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - Presenting a complex causal system as a linear text list or simple bullet points.

### Category 3: Learner Calibration
*Focus: Adjusting complexity based on prior knowledge.*

### MM-G4. Expertise-Reversal Calibration
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Learner Calibration
- **Definition:** Use simplified graphics (diagrams and illustrations) for novice learners; transition to perceptually rich or complex graphics only for high-knowledge learners.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Scale visual complexity based on the audience's prior knowledge.
- **Rationale:** Visuals with too many nonessential details (seductive details) can distract novices from essential components, decreasing visual saliency. Experts possess the "mental hooks" to organize complex data internally, but novices don’t.
- **Boundary Condition:** An exception for expert reversal — regardless of the learner's expertise, abstract images can be successfully used if the goal is to generate abstract feelings rather than showing physical features/functions.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - Using simple line drawings to teach a heart model to beginners.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - Using highly detailed, realistic, and shaded 3D models for a student's first introduction to a concept. Novice students will not be able to easily identify relevant parts/details in the image.

### MM-G5. Concrete vs. Abstract Visual Scaffolding
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Learner Calibration
- **Definition:** Provide concrete (perceptually accurate) and abstract (symbolic) representations for the same content when possible.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Start with an image that directly and closely represents or illustrates a content being presented and transition to a symbolic image to bridge the conceptual gap.
- **Rationale:** Starting with concrete visuals and transitioning to abstract ones supports optimal knowledge transfer during initial learning.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Expert reversal — for experts, presenting all or too many direct details can create extraneous cognitive load as experts do not need them to understand.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - Electrical engineering materials that show both photos of real components and their corresponding circuit symbols.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - Providing only abstract symbols to a novice who lacks the prerequisite knowledge to decode them.

### Category 4: Media Format & Delivery
*Focus: Matching the visual style to the type of knowledge.*

### MM-G6. Dynamic vs. Static Graphics
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Media Format & Delivery
- **Definition:** Use animation/video for procedural and motor skills where motion is critical. Use static graphics (pictures, diagrams, charts, graphs) for comparisons or conceptual systems where the learner should control the pace.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Animate for "How it moves"; use static images for "How they compare."
- **Rationale:** Animations are most effective when they explicitly represent dynamic behaviors that are difficult to mentally animate.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Expert reversal — for experts, static images or standard animations (without slow motion) work better.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - Using a video model to teach knot-tying or first aid techniques.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - Forcing expert learners to watch a slow animation of a process they can easily reason through via a static image.

### MM-G7. Direct vs. Symbolic Representation
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Media Format & Delivery
- **Definition:** Use direct graphic representations (illustrations, diagrams, animations) that directly or closely illustrate concrete explanations (especially for unfamiliar topics). Use symbolic/analogical images only to produce psychological effects or represent abstract concepts for familiar topics.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Novices need to see what it *looks* like; experts can handle what it *indirectly* represents.
- **Rationale:** Symbolic images can mislead novices who have not yet formed an accurate mental model of the subject. Novices still need to see what it looks like and how it works in order for them to create an accurate mental model, which requires direct graphic representations. Symbolic visualization involves guessing, and too much of it is counteractive to learning.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Expert reversal — too many direct details can create extraneous load for experts.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - Using a direct diagram of a heart when discussing blood circulation for novice students.
    - Using a real-life picture of the new food pyramid when discussing a balanced diet for novice students.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - Using a "metaphorical" image for a technical concept a student has never seen before.

### Category 5: Technical Quality & Impact
*Focus: Ensuring the visual is legible and engaging.*

### MM-G8. Visual Fidelity & Clarity
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Technical Quality
- **Definition:** Ensure images are sharp, clear, and large enough to show relevant details.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Assume students are viewing on small mobile screens; ensure details are visible and salient without full-screen mode.
- **Rationale:** Small images where relevant details are too small and hard to see as well as blurry or pixelated images create extraneous cognitive processing as students struggle to decode the visual, detracting from learning. These images are not impactful or memorable compared to large and clear images.
- **Boundary Conditions:** None mentioned.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - Showing a close-up video of a mouth to demonstrate pronunciation.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - Stretching a low-resolution thumbnail found online, resulting in a pixelated and illegible graphic.

### MM-G9. Visual Impact
- **Priority:** Low
- **Category:** Technical Quality
- **Definition:** Ensure images are clear and large enough to generate maximum emotional effects.
- **Rule of Thumb:** To entice the audience, make the image as large as possible, potentially filling the entire screen.
- **Rationale:** Large images are more enticing and memorable than "postage stamp" sized thumbnails.
- **Boundary Conditions:** If the image becomes pixelated when enlarged, the emotional impact is lost.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - Adjusting image size to be more like a "poster" than a "postage stamp" to increase psychological impact.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - Showing a small postage-stamp-size image on a title slide of your presentation slide deck.

# Redundancy Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** RDN

**Core Definition:** People learn better from graphics and narration than from graphics, narration, and printed text that repeats the narration. Redundancy occurs when the same information is presented concurrently in multiple forms (visual text and auditory speech), which can overload the visual channel.

## Design Guidelines

### RDN-G1. Avoid Concurrent Narration and Identical Text
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Cognitive Load Management)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Redundancy Principle
- **Definition:** Do not present on-screen text that is a word-for-word duplicate of the spoken narration when a graphic is also present.
- **Rule of Thumb:** If you are showing a graphic and explaining it via audio, do not include a transcript of that audio on the same screen.
- **Rationale:** When learners try to read text and watch a graphic simultaneously, they overload their visual channel. Additionally, the brain must exert cognitive effort to "match" the slightly different speeds of reading vs. hearing, creating extraneous load. Eliminating redundant text frees the visual channel to focus entirely on the imagery.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This rule applies primarily when a **graphic** is on screen. If there is no graphic, narration and text can sometimes be used together, though this is less effective than using a graphic.
    - You may use short printed keywords (1–3 words) next to parts of a graphic to act as a signal, provided they are not long enough to distract from the visual.
    - When there are no accompanying graphics and a narration and an onscreen text perfectly sync (like a closed caption), it will not cause extraneous cognitive processing although words only is generally not as effective as a combination of words and pictures.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A video showing a heart beating with a narrator explaining the valve function, with no on-screen captions or text blocks.
    - An educational slide where a diagram is explained via audio only, with no text at the bottom of the screen.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A slide showing a complex diagram while the narrator reads a paragraph of text that is also displayed verbatim on the screen.
    - Captions that cannot be turned off and mirror the audio exactly during a fast-moving animation.

### RDN-G2. Use Printed Text for Non-Native Speakers & Accessibility
- **Priority:** Medium (Strategic Support)
- **Category:** Individual Differences / Accessibility
- **Definition:** Provide on-screen text (captions/subtitles) specifically for learners who have low familiarity with the spoken language or for those with hearing impairments.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Make redundant text optional (toggle-able) rather than permanent.
- **Rationale:** For non-native speakers, the cognitive load of decoding spoken language is high; seeing the printed word helps them ground the phonemes, outweighing the redundancy cost.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Only beneficial if the learner actually needs the support. For native speakers, this remains a violation.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An English-language engineering video that allows users to toggle "Closed Captions" on or off based on their language proficiency.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Hard-coding subtitles into a video intended for a native-speaking audience where complex visuals are the primary focus.

### RDN-G3. Use Short Labels for Scannability
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Spatial Contiguity / Signaling
- **Definition:** Use short, 2–3 word labels or keywords to reinforce the key concepts while narrating rather than typing out the whole sentences.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Labels and keywords are not "redundant text"; they are "signposts" that a learner can recognize and identify at a glance without having to make a conscious reading comprehension effort.
- **Rationale:** Short labels and keywords increase the content scannability and help the learner organize the visual field without requiring the extensive reading time that a full sentence would.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Labels should be kept minimal. If a label becomes a full sentence, it triggers the Redundancy Effect. When providing a definition of a special terminology, the definition can be fully typed out.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Just list three keywords “cheaper,” “easier,” and “faster” when you talk about three value propositions of Walmart.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Typing a full sentence: "Walmart has three value propositions: cheaper, easier, and faster.”

### RDN-G4. Text for High-Technicality & New Terminology
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Pre-training / Cognitive Support
- **Definition:** Display a word on-screen if it is a highly technical, unfamiliar, or "math-heavy" term that is hard to mentally visualize.
- **Rule of Thumb:** If a student is likely to ask, "How do you spell that?"—put it on the screen.
- **Rationale:** Highly technical words impose a high "intrinsic load." Seeing the spelling reduces the effort required to identify the word, freeing up resources for understanding the concept.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Display the term briefly or as a label rather than as part of a long text block.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - The word "Desoxyribonucleic acid" appearing on screen for 5 seconds when it is first introduced in a biology lecture.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Narrating a simple, familiar list (e.g., "Apple, Orange, Banana") while also showing the list on screen.

### RDN-G5. Omit Unnecessary Explanatory Elaborations
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Cognitive Load Management)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Redundancy Principle
- **Definition:** Use abridged or summarized versions of text such as short bullet points rather than full-length, elaborated paragraphs or descriptions.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use concise captions or key phrases instead of original full-length sentences or paragraphs if the core message is clear. Suspect guideline violations when there is high text density.
- **Rationale:** Once a main point is understood, additional elaboration unnecessarily requires working memory resources to coordinate the new information with existing knowledge, while providing no further functional benefit, thus interfering with learning.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This applies specifically when the main points of the instructional material are already intelligible to the learner.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A "minimal manual" for software that provides only the essential steps for completing a task.
    - An illustrated summary of a science lesson that uses concise captions instead of the original full text.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Requiring students to read a full geography chapter when they learn the material better from a summary that is 20% of the length.
    - Including additional concrete, but non-essential, information in a mathematical word problem.

### RDN-G6. Exclude Redundant Text from Self-Contained Graphics
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Redundancy Principle
- **Definition:** Avoid adding written textual descriptions to diagrams or graphics that are already self-contained and easily understood through existing labels or arrows.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Choose either spoken words or written words to accompany a graphic, but do not integrate explanatory sentences that repeat what is already visually evident.
- **Rationale:** Integrated redundant onscreen text is difficult for a learner to avoid reading, forcing unnecessary and demanding mental coordination between the onscreen text and the graphic elements.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This rule applies to graphics that are already "self-contained and intelligible" through their own design elements.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A flow diagram of the water cycle presented without any accompanying explanatory text.
    - A single, clear set of diagrams illustrating a paper-folding task without added textual instructions.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - An intelligible diagram of blood flow in the heart that includes integrated sentences repeating the info already shown by arrows.
    - Adding multiple sets of diagrams showing different perspectives of the same simple task.

# Coherence Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** COH

**Core Definition:** People learn more deeply when extraneous material (words, pictures, or sounds) is excluded from a multimedia lesson rather than included. This framework is designed to reduce "Type 1 extraneous overload" by ensuring that only essential instructional material occupies the learner's limited cognitive capacity.

## Design Guidelines

### COH-G1. Weeding Out Seductive Details
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Cognitive Load Management)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Coherence Principle
- **Definition:** Eliminating interesting but instructionally irrelevant words and pictures that distract from the core learning goal.
- **Rule of Thumb:** If a graphic or a piece of text does not directly support the specific learning objective, remove it, no matter how "engaging" it seems.
- **Rationale:** Irrelevant material competes for limited working memory resources and can misguide the learner's attention away from essential content. While seductive details may increase "emotional interest," research consistently shows they significantly decrease "cognitive understanding."
- **Boundary Conditions:** For highly expert learners, some "visual flavoring" is less damaging because they have the internal schemata to filter it out; however, for novices, it is highly detrimental.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A video about lightning formation that uses only simple, labeled diagrams showing the movement of electrical charges.
    - A slide that removes decorative "clipart" to focus entirely on a central data chart.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Including a "fun fact" sidebar about the history of the raincoat in a technical video about the physics of thunderstorms.
    - Adding high-resolution, irrelevant background images behind an instructional text block.
    - Inserting a story about a high school football player struck by lightning during a technical physics lesson.

### COH-G2. Talking Head Management (Video Specific)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Media Format / Seductive Details
- **Definition:** Removing the instructor's "talking head" video window when complex visual aids (diagrams, charts, animations) are being explained.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Toggle the instructor's face off when the visual content requires the learner's full attention; keep the face visible only for "rapport-building" segments.
- **Rationale:** The human face is an extremely salient visual cue; keeping it on screen while explaining a diagram acts as a "seductive detail" that competes for visual attention, causing split-attention.
- **Boundary Conditions:** The face may remain on screen during purely verbal segments (introductions/summaries) to establish **Social Agency** and rapport. Adding a speaker’s talking head video to prime social learning effects is acceptable when the primary instructional content requires relatively low cognitive processing because the social learning benefit can outweigh the negative effect of the extraneous cognitive processing caused by the speaker’s talking head video.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - The video transitions to a full-screen view of a chemical reaction diagram the moment the instructor begins describing the process.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A small video of the instructor remains in the corner of the screen throughout a 10-minute walkthrough of a detailed historical timeline.

### COH-G3. Exclusion of Extraneous Sounds
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Auditory Processing / Coherence Principle
- **Definition:** Remove or reduce background music, environmental sounds, or opening and ending music/song that do not directly support the instructional goal.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Avoid background music, especially music with lyrics, during segments that contain spoken narration. If a music or song is used for the video opening, its duration prior to the real content should be kept really short (less than 5 seconds).
- **Rationale:** Background music can interfere with the processing of spoken words in the auditory channel, leading to cognitive overload. The brain processes every item it hears; background sounds are only permissible if they directly support the instructional goal rather than acting as "bells and whistles".
- **Boundary Conditions:** Music can be used during "visual-only" segments (like a time-lapse) or as an intro/outro to signal transitions. Consistent noises (white noise, instrumental music) are much less distracting and the brain learns to ignore them quickly, so they can be used to muffle other distracting noises in an environment where there are a lot of random noises.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A math tutorial recorded in a quiet environment with no background music during the explanation of formulas.
    - A music or song is used only for the opening of a video, and its duration is kept short (less than 5 seconds).
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Using "upbeat" background music with lyrics throughout a lecture on economic theory.
    - Adding "thunder sounds" to a computer-based lesson on the physics of lightning.
    - Background music playing continuously throughout a technical lecture.
    - A music or song is used only for the opening of a video, but its duration before the real content begins is way too long (10-40 seconds).

### COH-G4. Concise Content Delivery
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Video Engagement / Conciseness
- **Definition:** Tightly editing narration and visuals to remove redundancy and unnecessary elaborations.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Prioritize succinct content presentation over length. Keep lessons concise, focusing only on the words and pictures strictly needed to achieve the instructional objective.
- **Rationale:** Learners prefer succinct content presentation; shorter, tighter videos maintain higher engagement independent of total length. Extraneous material is most damaging when it is likely to increase cognitive load beyond the learner’s capacity; conciseness ensures material remains within working memory limits.
- **Boundary Conditions:** A long paragraph can be presented to provide an established definition or a screenshot of a textual material (e.g., Table, Figure).
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Using short, focused descriptions of a mechanical system rather than an embellished paragraph.
    - A lesson that provides a concise overview of earthquake faults and plate motion.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - An "embellished" lesson that includes additional non-essential technical details about a physical system.
    - An instructor "rambling" or telling irrelevant personal anecdotes during a technical walkthrough.

### VOC-G5. Concise Vocal Delivery
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Coherence Principle
- **Definition:** Tightly editing the audio to remove filler words ("ums," "ahs") and distracting mouth noises (clicks, heavy breaths). Remove "filler" words that slow down the effective speaking rate.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Clean audio is "invisible" audio. Any sound that isn't the instructional content is extraneous noise.
- **Rationale:** Filler words and mouth noises act as **Auditory Noise**. They force the learner to use extra cognitive resources to filter out the "garbage" from the "message," leading to faster fatigue.
- **Boundary Conditions:** 
    - Do not edit so aggressively that you remove necessary pauses required for the learner to "digest" complex information (segmenting).
    - Do not over-edit to the point where the voice lacks natural breathing, as this can trigger the machine-like voice penalty.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Removing unscripted “ums” and “ahs” between sentences to maintain a crisp, energetic speaking rate of 185–254 wpm.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A recording with significant background hiss, microphone pops, or frequent audible lip smacks.

### COH-G6. Visual Fidelity and Impact
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Technical Clarity)
- **Category:** Technical Quality / Coherence Principle
- **Definition:** Adjusting image size, resolution, and style to ensure that important details are clear and produce a high psychological impact.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Design images to function like a "poster" (large, clear, and impactful) rather than a "postage stamp" (small, cluttered, or illegible thumbnail).
- **Rationale:** If visuals are small or blurry, the learner must exert extra effort to "decode" the image before they can even begin to "process" the instruction. This creates extraneous cognitive processing that directly detracts from learning.
- **Boundary Conditions:** High fidelity does not always mean high realism. Realistic photos can sometimes contain "too much realism" (seductive details) that conceals core concepts. In these cases, a simple, high-resolution drawing is superior to a photograph.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Using a large, sharp image that remains clear and legible even when the video is not being played in full-screen mode.
    - Selecting a clean, high-resolution drawing over a cluttered, realistic photo to illustrate a specific procedure like tying a belt.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Using "postage stamp" sized thumbnails that make critical details impossible to distinguish.
    - Stretching a small, low-resolution image until it becomes pixelated and creates a distracting visual "noise."

### COH-G7. Minimize Spoken-Written Words Inconsistency
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Cognitive Load Management)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Coherence Principle
- **Definition:** Spoken and written words do not deviate from each other significantly most of the time especially in fast-pacing presentations.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use the same words, phrases, and sentences for both spoken texts and written texts.
- **Rationale:** If you use written texts that significantly differ from your spoken texts, learners will have hard times finding the corresponding texts on the screen. If a video is fast-paced, a learner may not have enough time to find the corresponding texts. Learners will also have to exert extra cognitive resources to interpret and resolve the spoken-written text gaps/inconsistency, making it hard to keep up with the video content.
- **Boundary Conditions:** With slow-paced videos and simple concepts, negotiating/resolving the verbal gaps/inconsistency may promote knowledge generative processing.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - The speaker and onscreen texts use the same key phrases.
    - The spoken texts and corresponding written texts use the same or similar sentence structures.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - The speaker and onscreen texts use completely different key phrases (synonyms) — e.g., the speaker says, “Do not create a gap” and the onscreen text says, “Avoid inconsistency.”
    - The spoken texts and corresponding written texts use significantly different sentence structures — e.g., the speaker says, “Textual inconsistency can cause extraneous cognitive processing” and the onscreen text says, “Extraneous cognitive processing is produced when there’s textual inconsistency.”

### COH-G8. Ensure Good Speech Audio Quality and Adequate Volume
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Auditory Processing / Coherence Principle
- **Definition:** Ensure the good vocal sound quality and adequate volume for comfortable listening.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use good audio recording devices and settings for clear and easy listening. Enhance speech audio in post-editing if the original recording has a poor quality.
- **Rationale:** Poor vocal recording quality and low recording volume interfere with the processing of spoken words in the auditory channel, leading to cognitive overload.
- **Boundary Conditions:** None.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Voiceover narration sounds clear and easy to listen to like a studio-produced podcast.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Voiceover narration sounds muffled.
    - The volume of voiceover narration is too quiet and faint most of the time.

### COH-G9. Exclusion of Content Not Covered via Narration
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Coherence Principle
- **Definition:** Remove onscreen contents not talked about in the narration, leaving only the onscreen contents directly covered through the narration.
- **Rule of Thumb:** If it is not talked about through narration, don’t place it on screen/slide.
- **Rationale:** Visuals or onscreen contents are “visual aids” used to augment spoken contents. Unmentioned onscreen contents clutter the screen and become extraneous elements that learners will have to sift through, making it harder to focus on the relevant information on the screen.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Extra information can be added to the screen if the narration acknowledges it and instructs students to study it on their own.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Data about only the U.S. is extracted and presented on the screen from the source that contains data from 10 different countries because the lecture only concerns the U.S. data.
    - A complete list of survey results is presented on screen, and the narration says to pause and read through the list or read it later.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A complete list of survey results is presented on screen, but the narration only talks about just one of the results.

# Spatial Contiguity Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** SPC

**Core Definition:** People learn better when corresponding words and pictures are presented near rather than far from each other on the page or screen. This reduces "Type 2 extraneous overload" by ensuring learners do not waste cognitive energy on visual searching.

## Design Guidelines

### SPC-G1. Physical Integration of Text and Visuals
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Cognitive Load Management)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Spatial Contiguity
- **Definition:** Embedding explanatory notes, solution steps, or captions directly within or immediately adjacent to their corresponding graphics (pictures, diagrams, and graphs).
- **Rule of Thumb:** Place printed words near the specific part of the graphics they describe; avoid "caption blocks" located at the bottom of a slide.
- **Rationale:** Physical integration acts as a "sensory bundle," reducing the cognitive effort required to scan back and forth. This eliminates the need for "representational holding," where a learner must remember text while searching for its visual match.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This rule applies primarily when the text and visuals are mutually dependent (neither is fully intelligible alone). If the text is merely redundant and long, integration may trigger the "Redundancy Effect."
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - **Geometry Worked Examples:** Solution steps embedded directly into a triangle diagram at the exact locations where the goal angles are found.
    - **Biology Illustrations:** A heart diagram where the descriptions of blood flow are integrated into the visual paths rather than in a separate paragraph.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A technical manual where the text instructions are on page 5 but the corresponding diagrams are on page 10.
    - Placing Pinyin translations for Chinese characters in a separate horizontal list to the right, rather than directly under each character.

### SPC-G2. Elimination of Legends and Separated Keys
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Spatial Contiguity
- **Definition:** Replacing numbered legends or letter-coded keys with direct, on-graphic labels.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Instead of a "Figure 1" legend at the bottom of a slide, place labels inside or immediately adjacent to the graphic.
- **Rationale:** Legends force the eye to move between the data and the key, creating a "split-attention effect" that significantly increases extraneous cognitive load.
- **Boundary Conditions:** If the diagram is extremely high in detail and adding labels would cause "Visual Clutter," use the **Signaling Strategy** (see SPC-G4) instead.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - **Data Visualization:** A pie chart where category labels (e.g., "Sales") and percentages are placed **inside** the slices rather than in a side legend.
    - **Interactive Callouts:** Placing the label "Aorta" directly next to the vessel in a heart diagram with a pointer.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A diagram that uses numbers (1, 2, 3) with a corresponding legend at the bottom of the slide to define those numbers.
    - A bar graph that uses a legend to indicate labels for the bars in the graph.
    - A physics chart that uses a color-coded key to identify different forces, requiring the student to look back and forth to decode the colors.

### SPC-G3. Text-Graphic Gap Minimization
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Spatial Contiguity
- **Definition:** Minimizing the spatial distance between a text element and its corresponding graphic referent on the digital screen.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Place on-screen text immediately next to the relevant section of a graphic rather than at a remote location like the screen margins.
- **Rationale:** Even subtle spatial separation forces the learner to expend cognitive resources to assimilate the information. Reducing visual scanning eliminates a specific form of extraneous processing.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This is most critical when the content is **high in "element interactivity"** (many parts working together). For very simple tasks, split-attention might not reach the threshold of cognitive overload.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Placing the names of the body parts right next to the corresponding parts in the picture.
    - **E-Learning UI:** Inserting instructional tooltips at the exact interaction points within the software interface.
    - **Language Learning:** Placing Pinyin translations in a vertical format directly beneath the corresponding Chinese characters.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A picture of human muscular anatomy has all the parts numbered in the picture, and the numbers and their muscle part names are listed at the bottom of the anatomy picture.
    - A "separated presentation" where the text is displayed on the left half of the screen and the diagram is on the right half.
    - Placing the description of a specific machine part at the top of a page while the machine diagram sits at the bottom.

### SPC-G4. Strategic Attention-Directing Cues (Alternative Strategy)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Attention Guidance / Signaling
- **Definition:** Using signaling techniques—such as color-highlighting, circling, underlining, arrows, and spot-lighting—to highlight the relevant parts of the screen such as the text and its corresponding graphic when full physical integration is impossible.
- **Rule of Thumb:** When the narration talks about some parts of the screen, synchronize visual markups (color highlight, circling, underlining, spot-lighting, pointing with an arrow, etc.) to direct the learner’s attention to these parts.
- **Rationale:** Signaling serves as a visual guide that directs the gaze, reducing the need for extensive visual searching when the screen is crowded.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Use this as a "Plan B." Physical integration is always preferred; signaling should only be used if the screen content (picture, diagram, graph, table, etc.) is too complex or cluttered for embedded labels.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A narration talks about a line of code, and the corresponding code segment immediately gets highlighted.
    - A narration talks about certain parts of a line graph, and the corresponding parts of the graph is highlighted using a spotlight effect.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Showing a complex diagram without using dynamic cueing methods (fade-in, highlighting, circling, pointing with arrow, etc.) and expecting the student to find the correct component based on the narration alone.
    - Static markups (color-highlights, arrows, shapes, etc.) are already embedded in the screen content, but they are present the entire time from the beginning, so they don’t help viewers focus on the relevant items at the relevant times.

### SPC-G5. Content Complexity Assessment
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Learner Calibration / Expertise Reversal
- **Definition:** Apply spatial integration by evaluating the "element interactivity" of a task to determine if spatial integration is pedagogically imperative.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Apply spatial integration especially when sources are unintelligible in isolation.
- **Rationale:** If sources are redundant (merely repeating each other), integration is not imperative while it’s still OK to do. For simple tasks, the brain can handle split-attention without overload.
- **Boundary Conditions:** **Expertise Reversal Effect:** Providing integrated explanatory text to high-knowledge learners can actually hinder their learning, as they already possess the mental models to organize the information.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - **Complex Spreadsheet Instruction:** Integrating formula logic directly into a screen capture of a complicated Excel function.
    - **Physics Manipulations:** Integrating formula statements with the substitution and manipulation steps in a mechanics problem.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - **Redundant Integration:** Physically integrating a diagram with a text description that simply says "This is a picture of a heart".
    - **Expert Instruction:** Forcing an experienced surgeon to read integrated labels on a basic anatomical chart they have already mastered.

# Temporal Contiguity Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** TMP

**Core Definition:** People learn better when corresponding words and pictures are presented simultaneously rather than successively. This ensures that learners do not have to waste cognitive energy on "representational holding"—the taxing effort of keeping one piece of information in mind while waiting for its corresponding part to appear.

## Design Guidelines

### TMP-G1. Synchronous Audio-Visual Delivery
- **Priority:** High (Fundamental for temporal alignment)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Temporal Contiguity
- **Definition:** Presenting spoken words at the exact same time the corresponding visual (onscreen texts or graphics) event or transformation appears on the screen.
- **Rule of Thumb:** The explanation must occur *as* the corresponding visual (onscreen text or graphic) is shown, not before or after. Inevitably, visual presentation must progressively show fewer items at a time instead of showing all items at the same time.
- **Rationale:** When spoken words and visuals (onscreen texts and graphics) are simultaneous, they are more likely to be in working memory at the same time. This facilitates the construction of mental connections between verbal, sound, and pictorial representations (multimodal convergence/integration). If visuals are static (all visual items are on the screen the entire time), matching spoken words with the specific corresponding visuals becomes harder.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Successive presentation or use of static slide (showing all items at the same time instead of progressively showing fewer items at a time) is less detrimental if the material is simple (low element interactivity) or if the learner has high prior knowledge (Expertise Reversal Effect).
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A narrator says, "The inlet valve opens," at the exact moment the animation depicts the valve opening.
    - In a language learning app, the audio of a word plays at the exact moment the corresponding image appears.
    - Narration talks about ten different items, and ten corresponding bullet points are presented one bullet at a time perfectly synced with the narration.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Providing a full narrative description of a bicycle pump’s operation *before* showing a silent animation of the pump.
    - Showing a diagram on one slide and providing the spoken explanation on the next slide.
    - Narration talks about ten different items, and all ten corresponding onscreen texts are on screen from the beginning.

### TMP-G2. Temporal Gap Minimization
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Temporal Contiguity
- **Definition:** Ensuring there is no significant time lag (ideally less than 7 seconds) between related auditory and visual (onscreen text or graphic) components. Visual material should not be presented much earlier or later than its voiceover.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Visual material (onscreen text or graphic) and its voiceover should overlap completely or occur within a 7-second window. Visual material should not be presented more than 7 seconds earlier or later than its voiceover.
- **Rationale:** Research indicates that even small gaps (7 to 21 seconds) are detrimental. Presenting visual material much later than its voiceover forces the brain to "hold" the voiceover content in memory while attending to the visual, which exceeds sensory memory limits. Presenting visual material much earlier than its voiceover clutters the screen, which creates extraneous cognitive load to match the spoken words with visuals.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Brief delays (1–2 seconds) may be acceptable if they serve as a "signaling" technique to direct the learner's eye before the explanation begins, provided it doesn't tax representational holding.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Ensuring the voiceover for a chart begins within 2 seconds of the chart appearing on screen.
    - Audio and video segments that are precisely trimmed to start and end together.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A film where the auditory component is delayed by 21 seconds after the visuals have already disappeared.
    - Presenting a complex visual 14 seconds *before* the corresponding auditory explanation begins.
    - All slide contents are on screen the entire time with no visual cues or fade-in/appear animation effects while narration talks about them.

### TMP-G3. Strategic Successive Segmentation
- **Priority:** Medium (Mitigation strategy)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Temporal Contiguity
- **Definition:** Dividing information into very short, successive "mini-segments" if simultaneous presentation is technically or logistically impossible.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Break the sequence into one or two lines of narration followed immediately by the corresponding visual action.
- **Rationale:** When episodes are very short, the working memory load is low enough that learners can integrate the information themselves even without perfect synchronization.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This is a "Plan B." Simultaneous delivery is always superior for complex, high-interactivity content. Segmentation is only used to mitigate the damage of successive presentation.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Dividing a long lesson on lightning into 16 mini-segments where short bursts of narration and animation alternate.
    - A step-by-step tutorial where each 5-second video clip is followed immediately by its 5-second audio description.
    - A series of static slides, each of which contains only 1-2 topics without dynamic visual cues.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Presenting a 140-second continuous narration followed by a 140-second continuous animation.
    - Playing a full 2-minute animation of the water cycle in silence, followed by a 2-minute oral summary.
    - A single static slide that contains visuals for multiple topics without dynamic visual cues.

### TMP-G4. Coordination of Transient Information
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Temporal Contiguity
- **Definition:** Avoiding the displacement of information that disappears quickly (transient info like speech or animation) from the visuals it describes.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Ensure the static visual anchor remains present while the transient audio describes it.
- **Rationale:** The "transient information effect" occurs when a spoken sentence disappears before the learner can process it. If the related visual also disappears or is displaced in time, the total load overwhelms the learner.
- **Boundary Conditions:** If the learner has **Control of Pacing** (e.g., a "Pause" or "Replay" button), the danger of transient information loss is significantly mitigated.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Narrating a diagram in real-time so the transient speech is supported by a permanent on-screen visual.
    - The voice says "brakes are applied" at the same moment the animation shows the brake pedal moving.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Showing a high-complexity animation and then removing it from the screen *before* providing the spoken explanation.
    - A video where the narrator talks about a chart that hasn't appeared on screen yet.
    - A video shows an animation of the brake pedal moving, but the narrator doesn’t talk about it until much later.

### TMP-G5. Pace Matching and Learner Control
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Temporal Contiguity
- **Definition:** Ensuring the speed and timing of the narration matches the speed and timing of the visual transformation or appearance/disappearance.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Tight temporal synchronization between audio and corresponding visuals is required for system-controlled (automated) lessons.
- **Rationale:** Matching pace ensures that information is integrated the moment it enters working memory, minimizing the time it must be "held" or “searched.”
- **Boundary Conditions:** On **learner-paced static slides** (where the user clicks "Next"), the Temporal Contiguity Principle is less critical because the learner controls the integration time. This allows them to mentally animate the system at their own speed.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Fast-paced, system-controlled videos with audio and video tracks that are perfectly aligned in the editing timeline.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A slideshow where the text slide is displayed first, and the corresponding diagram is shown only after the user clicks "Next".
    - A video where the narration is significantly faster than the animation, causing the audio to "get ahead" of the visual referent.
    - A video where all the visuals are already on screen before the narrator talks about them, making the viewer have to search and find the relevant visuals as the narrator talks about them.

# Signaling Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** SIG

**Core Definition:** People learn better when cues that highlight the organization of the essential material are added. Signaling (or cueing) reduces extraneous cognitive load by directing the learner's attention to the most relevant information at the correct time. This is particularly vital for novices navigating complex displays, as it helps them bypass extraneous processing and focus directly on task-relevant information.

## Design Guidelines

### SIG-G1. Visual Attentional Cueing (Arrows & Indicators)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Signaling Principle
- **Definition:** Using extrinsic visual indicators—such as arrows, circles, or flashing elements—to point to relevant graphic elements as they are mentioned especially when there are a few other items on the screen.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use visual indicators like arrows or "spotlighting" to direct the eye to the specific part of a visual (onscreen text and graphic) being discussed in real-time.
- **Rationale:** Novices often focus on perceptually salient but irrelevant features; perfectly timed cues redirect their attention to the task-relevant data exactly when it is needed for processing.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This is most effective when the visual display is complex. On very simple slides, adding arrows can become extraneous and violate the Coherence Principle. Over-signaling (too many cues at once) can create "visual clutter," which increases rather than decreases extraneous load.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A bright red circle appearing around the "Intake Valve" of an engine at the exact moment the narrator mentions it.
    - Using a bright red arrow to point to a specific button during a software tutorial.
    - Dreading (dimming) the background of a complex spreadsheet and using a "spotlight" to illuminate only the cell containing a specific formula.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A complex animation of a jet engine where the narrator mentions "the fuel injector" without any visual indicator on the screen.
    - Talking about a specific data point in a dense scatter plot without any visual indication of its location.
    - Using inconsistent cues (e.g., using a green box for "danger" and a red circle for "success").
    - A map showing hundreds of data points without any highlights or circles to show the specific area being discussed.

### SIG-G2. Pacing with Progressive Presentation
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Signaling Principle
- **Definition:** Showing screen/slide items progressively using animation effects—such as fade-in, appear, or undimming slide items—to synchronize the spoken words and the delivery of corresponding visual onscreen items.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use animation effects like “fade-in” or “appear” to show the onscreen items as they are mentioned.
- **Rationale:** When the screen or slide is dense with contents or texts, the learner has to make an extra effort to search and find the relevant items on the screen that correspond to the narration. By showing the exact item as it is presented eliminates the need for visual search, reducing cognitive load.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This is most effective when multiple items need to be presented on screen or slide one at a time. On very simple slides where all items are discussed quickly and briefly, animating each item can become extraneous and violate the Coherence Principle. Over-signaling (too many cues at once) can create "visual clutter," which increases rather than decreases extraneous load.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A slide that has a list of 10 bullet points that are presented with the fade-in effect as the narration talks about them.
    - Bullet points are initially dimmed (grayed) and get undimmed as they are discussed with the narration.
    - A road map or timeline diagram with many complex components uses the “fade-in” presentation effect so that individual components/parts are shown as they are discussed through the narration.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A slide that has a list of 10 bullet points that are not animated or highlighted with visual cues as each bullet point is discussed.
    - An entire road map or timeline is shown from the beginning even though it is complex and contains a lot of information.

### SIG-G3. Dynamic Signaling (Progressive Path Cueing)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Animation Principle
- **Definition:** Using moving visual cues—such as a "pulse" of light or a traveling ribbon of color—to guide the eye along a functional causal or process chain.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use progressive path cues (the cues that move/travel through a path) rather than static arrows that stay in one place when explaining a process that involves movement or energy flow.
- **Rationale:** Static cues often cannot compete with the powerful attention-directing effect of dynamic contrast in an animation. Progressive path cueing recruits that same dynamic contrast to ensure the learner's eye follows the most instructionally relevant path.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This is most critical for complex systems; for simple linear processes, static cues may be sufficient.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A moving "glow" that travels through a series of pipes to show the exact path of liquid in a hydraulic system.
    - A colored line that progressively draws the path of an electrical current through a circuit diagram.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Relying on simple, static arrow cues in a high-speed animation where the eye is too busy following the motion to see the arrows.

### SIG-G4. Verbal Signaling (Narration Directives)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Social Agency / Verbal Guidance
- **Definition:** Using spoken "signposts" in the narration to tell the learner exactly where to look, when to look, and what to notice.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Reconceptualize the narration as a "tour guide" for the eyes (e.g., "Notice the blue lever on the left...").
- **Rationale:** Because visual perception is highly selective, learners often attend to salient but irrelevant features. Spoken directives serve as a corrective force to align the learner's focus with instructional relevance.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Experts may find excessive verbal guidance redundant; however, it is essential for novices.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An instructor saying, "Look at the small spring in the top left as I press the button."
    - Utilizing an "animation narrative" that tells the learner exactly what to notice during a split-second interaction.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A narrator describing the "big picture" of a system while a complex, multi-part animation is running, providing no guidance on which specific part to observe first.

### SIG-G5. Structural Signaling (Headings & Outlines)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Content Organization / Scaffolding
- **Definition:** Using clear headings, outlines, and icons to reveal the underlying structure of the lesson.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use a consistent heading style and pair headings with unique icons to make different sections easily discernable.
- **Rationale:** Structural signals help the learner build a "mental scaffolding" of the lesson. This allows them to organize incoming information into a coherent framework more easily.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Do not use too many heading levels (limit to 3), as a deep hierarchy can become confusing to navigate. Structural signaling is most effective for long or high-complexity expository texts. For very short or simple lessons, these cues may become redundant and tax working memory unnecessarily.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A 10-minute video with topic headings displayed on screen at all times so that viewers can easily tell which topic is being delivered, which also makes it easier to scrub through the video to find relevant topics.
    - A video that starts with a "map" of the four topics to be covered and shows that map briefly between each transition.
    - Using a unique icon (e.g., a gear for "Process") next to every heading related to mechanical operation.
    - Using bold, descriptive headings before each transition in a technical software manual.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A 20-minute video with no headings or verbal transitions, making it difficult for the learner to tell when one topic ends and another begins.

### SIG-G6. Auditory and Visual Emphasis (Key Terms)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Multimedia Principle / Social Agency
- **Definition:** Highlighting essential vocabulary or concepts through visual formatting (bold/color) or auditory stress (intonation/pitch).
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use bold font for printed text and vocal emphasis for narrated words to signal importance.
- **Rationale:** Highlighting key terms reduces the extraneous cognitive effort learners must spend searching for the most important parts of a message.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Over-signaling can lead to the "seductive detail" effect, where nothing stands out and the learner becomes distracted by formatting.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Using red font for key mathematical terms in a worked-out solution to make variables stand out.
    - A narrator using deliberate vocal stress and pitch changes to highlight critical steps.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A text-heavy slide where technical terms are formatted identically to surrounding filler text.
    - Using a monotone digital voice with no intonation to narrate complex content.

### SIG-G7. Integrated Color Coding
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Spatial Contiguity / Signaling Principle
- **Definition:** Coordinating written text and illustrations by giving corresponding elements in both representations the same unique color.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Assign the same color to a keyword in the text and its corresponding part in the accompanying diagram.
- **Rationale:** Color coordination highlights the relationship between elements, reducing the cognitive load required to mentally map and integrate separate materials.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Avoid "color clutter" by using too many colors, and ensure colors do not conflict with existing domain conventions.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Giving the word "piston" in a text block the same red color as the piston in the technical drawing.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A biology diagram using various aesthetic colors that do not correlate with the black text labels.

### SIG-G8. Visual Saliency via Shading
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Attention Guidance / Signaling Principle
- **Definition:** Increasing the relative importance of relevant elements by dimming (shading) irrelevant background or peripheral information, especially when there’s more irrelevant background or peripheral information than the relevant elements.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use "spotlighting" by dimming peripheral information to emphasize a specific subsystem.
- **Rationale:** Enhances visual saliency without adding new extrinsic information (like arrows), helping learners select the correct information without increasing the number of elements they must process.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Shading must create contrast but not be so dark that essential background context is completely lost. This is one of the visual cueing methods. If a different type of visual cue is already used, you don’t need to use shading. Over-signaling (too many cues at once) can create "visual clutter," which increases rather than decreases extraneous load. The Simplicity Rule.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Dimming the background of a complex software interface to highlight a single active dialog box or menu.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Highlighting a small element with a thin outline while leaving a high-contrast background at full brightness.
    - Shading is not used to highlight relevant elements AND no other visual cues are used even when visual cues are desired.

### SIG-G9. Visual Saliency via Zooming
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Attention Guidance / Signaling Principle
- **Definition:** Increasing the relative importance of relevant elements by narrowing the field of view through zooming in on a specific focal area, especially when important details are small and hard to see.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Make peripheral information invisible or secondary by zooming in on the relevant part of a complex or busy display.
- **Rationale:** Zooming forces the learner’s attention directly to the specific detail being discussed, eliminating the cognitive load required to search a large visual field.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Ensure the "context" is not entirely lost; use transitions or picture-in-picture to show where the focal area fits within the larger system.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Zooming in on a specific region of a large geological map exactly as that area is mentioned in the narration.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Displaying a full, busy screen of a complex geological map throughout a 10-minute explanation without ever narrowing the field of view.

# Segmenting Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** SEG

## Core Principle

**Core Definition:** People learn better when a multimedia message is presented in user-paced segments rather than as a continuous unit. Segmenting (or "chunking") prevents cognitive overload by allowing the learner to fully process one "bite" of information before moving to the next.

The **Segmenting Principle** is your primary defense against "essential overload"—the state where the sheer complexity of the topic exceeds the learner's cognitive capacity. By breaking a continuous flow into learner-paced chunks, you allow the brain to fully process and "package" one piece of information before the next arrives.

These guidelines are structured to help distinguish between a "marathon lecture" and a "pedagogical sprint."

## Design Guidelines

### SEG-G1. Logical Chunking of Complex Content
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Cognitive Load Management)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Segmenting Principle
- **Definition:** Dividing long, continuous narrated videos into shorter, manageable segments based on logical steps or concepts.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Aim for segments that cover a single causal step or roughly 10–20 seconds of media if the topic is highly technical. Use one slide for one topic or subtopic. Do not place multiple topics or subtopics within the same slide especially if doing so makes the slide dense and cluttered.
- **Rationale:** If an explanation requires holding more than four items in working memory simultaneously for sense-making, it causes cognitive collapse. Segmenting slows the presentation pace to a level that allows for essential processing of each element.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Segmenting is only necessary when the material is high in "element interactivity" (complex/fast-paced). For very simple or short lessons (e.g., under 30 seconds total), further chunking provides no benefit.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Dividing a 140-second animation on lightning formation into 16 distinct, logical segments.
    - Presenting a complex pulley system animation in controllable micro-steps rather than a continuous 3-minute stream.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Three distinctively different subtopics (fintech definition, fintech.com, and fintech value proposition) are placed on the same slide, and the slide is dense and text-heavy because of that.
    - A 20-minute uninterrupted video lecture covering five different complex physics theories without any logical breaks.
    - A technical explanation of an electric motor delivered as a single, continuous unit.

### SEG-G2. The 6-Minute Rule (Micro-Learning)
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Video Engagement)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Video Engagement
- **Definition:** Dividing continuous instructional video into shorter, manageable segments or standalone videos to allow for mental consolidation.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Limit instructional videos to **6 minutes or less** for non-lecture content; for complex lectures, use a 15-minute cap only if high social presence is maintained.
- **Rationale:** Analysis of millions of video sessions proves that median engagement time is at most 6 minutes. After 9 minutes, student engagement drops by 50% regardless of the total video length. Because videos provide a continuous stream of information, learners can easily be overwhelmed. Segmenting material into 6-minute intervals provides "mental breaks" that allow the brain's working memory to process and integrate each chunk before moving on to the next.
- **Boundary Conditions:** For extremely low-complexity or motivational content, segments can be longer, but technical content (complex, multi-step, or high-density information) must strictly adhere to this limit.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A 30-minute lecture on cellular biology is divided into five separate 6-minute videos, each focused on a specific organelle.
    - A technical training module features "pause points" or interactive question slides every 4 to 5 minutes to ensure comprehension.
    - A technical software tutorial that is broken into 3-minute "concept capsules."
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Uploading a single, continuous 45-minute classroom recording without any timestamps, chapters, or built-in breaks.
    - A 15-minute physics tutorial that presents four different formulas in a single uninterrupted sequence.

### SEG-G3. User-Paced Interaction (The "Next" Button)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Interactivity
- **Definition:** Allowing the learner to control the pace of the lesson by clicking a "Next" button or using "Pause/Play" between segments.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Stop the animation or narration after a meaningful segment and require a learner action (e.g., clicking "Continue") to proceed. If the video platform is not capable of the “auto-stop” feature, at least incorporate couple-second pauses and visual signs into logical break points.
- **Rationale:** This allows the learner to finish the "essential processing" of the current segment before the "next" segment enters the sensory channel. It prevents the "transient information effect" where new info displaces old info before it is consolidated.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Expertise Reversal — Experts may find frequent pausing annoying as they can process larger chunks simultaneously.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A narrated animation of a lightning strike that pauses after the "stepped leader" phase, requiring the user to click "Play" to see the "return stroke."
    - A narrated video that pauses for a couple of seconds during logical break points before moving onto the next topic.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - An automated 10-minute video that covers a complex 20-step process without any pauses or interactive check-ins.

### SEG-G4. Navigation via Chaptering and Timestamps
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Video Engagement / Navigation
- **Definition:** Providing clear, clickable navigation points (chapters) within a single video file.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use YouTube-style timestamps or LMS-integrated chapters for any video longer than 6 minutes.
- **Rationale:** Students re-watching videos have a much higher dropout rate (78.6%) because they are usually looking for a specific segment. Chapters allow them to satisfy their information needs without the frustration of manual scrubbing.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Less critical for very short (<3 min) videos.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A 10-minute video with a "Table of Contents" in the description that links to specific start times for each sub-topic.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A long technical walkthrough where the learner must "guess" where a specific software setting is explained by dragging the playhead back and forth.

# Modality Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** MOD

**Core Definition:** People learn better from graphics and narration than from graphics and on-screen text. This principle focuses on expanding effective working memory by spreading the cognitive load across the brain's two primary processing systems: the phonological loop (auditory) and the visuospatial sketchpad (visual). By offloading verbal information to the ears, we leave the eyes free to do the heavy lifting of analyzing complex graphics.

## Design Guidelines

### MOD-G1. Auditory Offloading for Verbal Content
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Expanding Working Memory)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Modality Principle
- **Definition:** Presenting verbal explanations as spoken narration rather than on-screen text especially when they accompany a graphic or animation. Keep the onscreen texts concise and to minimum like key phrases.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Favor ears over eyes for words. Use the auditory channel for language to leave the visual channel free for imagery. Do not fill the screen with too much text such as long paragraphs or sentences.
- **Rationale:** Spreading information across two channels expands the total working memory capacity. If both text and graphics are visual, they compete for the same limited "sketchpad," leading to cognitive overload. In the worst case scenario, concurrent on-screen text "blinds" the learner to the graphic due to split-attention. Offloading verbal information to the ears leaves the eyes free to analyze complex graphics.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Use on-screen text if the graphics are very simple, if the audience has hearing impairments, if the learner is a non-native speaker, or if the technical environment lacks audio capability.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An educational animation where a narrator explains the steps of lightning formation as they happen visually.
    - Using a recorded voice to explain geometry problems paired with diagrams on a screen.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A silent video showing a chemical reaction with only written captions explaining the steps.
    - An animation of lightning formation paired with long printed text blocks at the bottom of the screen.
    - Presenting a fast-paced, system-controlled animation with concurrent on-screen text, forcing the brain to use only the verbal channel.

### MOD-G2. Complexity and Interactivity Calibration
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Task Management
- **Definition:** Prioritizing narration for instructional material with high "element interactivity," where multiple interconnected components must be processed at once. Do not use lengthy onscreen texts that are redundant with the spoken texts.
- **Rule of Thumb:** The modality effect is strongest when the material is complex and the presentation pace is fast.
- **Rationale:** Expanding working memory is only beneficial when capacity is actually strained. Simple material (low element interactivity) does not require dual-modality to be understood.
- **Boundary Conditions:** For simple instructional steps or a basic list of facts, text-only is often sufficient and avoids the unnecessary "transient information" risk associated with audio.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Using spoken explanations for complex geometry proofs involving multiple interconnected steps.
    - Auditory presentation of complex electrical circuit troubleshooting information.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Applying narration to a simple list of five independent facts that do not require visual integration.
    - Using audio for a single-step, low-load instruction that is easily read in a second.

### MOD-G3. Managing Transient Information Risks
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Information Persistence
- **Definition:** Limiting auditory-only delivery to relatively small, simple chunks of verbal information.
- **Rule of Thumb:** If the verbal information is essential but exceptionally long, technical, or unfamiliar, opt for permanent written text.
- **Rationale:** Spoken words are "transient"—they disappear once spoken. If information is too long or complex, the learner may forget the beginning of the sentence before they reach the end.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Written text is superior for second-language learners, technical terms with complex spellings, or when the learner needs to re-access the information indefinitely.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Providing a written list of 15 technical terms on a static slide for continuous reference.
    - Using short, narrated segments (10–15 seconds) to explain specific data points on a temperature graph.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Presenting a 2-minute, complex technical monologue as audio-only without any visual text support.
    - Using spoken words alone for a lengthy list of complex facts that exceed the learner's representational holding.

### MOD-G4. Visual Signaling and Cueing Integration
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Attention Guidance / Signaling
- **Definition:** Incorporating visual pointers (arrows, flashing, or spotlighting) to coordinate the audio narration with specific parts of the screen content (graphics and onscreen texts).
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use "pointing" cues to show exactly which part of the onscreen content (graphics and onscreen texts) is being described by the voice.
- **Rationale:** The modality effect is significantly enhanced when visual indicators (pointers) guide the learner's eyes, facilitating the mental integration of the auditory and visual streams.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Avoid over-cueing. If a graphic is simple enough that the referent is obvious, additional arrows become "seductive details" and violate the Coherence Principle.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Highlighting a specific line in a geometry display by flashing it at the moment the narrator mentions it.
    - Using a red arrow to point to the "inlet valve" in a motor animation during the spoken explanation.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A narrated animation of a jet engine where the narrator mentions a "piston" but the entire engine remains static and un-highlighted.
    - A narrator refers to a specific data on a data table, but no visual pointer/indicator is used to highlight the mentioned data.

### MOD-G5. Expertise and Learner Profile Calibration
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Learner Calibration / Expertise Reversal
- **Definition:** Adjusting the use of narration based on the learner's prior knowledge of the subject area.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Target novices with narration; be prepared to remove it for experts.
- **Rationale:** Inexperienced learners lack mental schemas and rely on dual-modality for support. For experts, narration of familiar content can become redundant and inhibit learning (Expertise Reversal Effect).
- **Boundary Conditions:** Even for experts, narration remains useful if the material is entirely new or if the specific visual-auditory integration is the goal of the lesson.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Providing full spoken narration for apprentices learning the basics of electrical engineering.
    - Using only diagrams for expert engineers who possess the schemas to process the visuals without verbal aid.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Forcing a high-experience professional to follow a slow-paced, mandatory audio narration of content they already master.

# Pre-training Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** PRE

**Core Definition:** People learn more deeply from a multimedia message when they know the names and characteristics of the main concepts. Pre-training reduces the "intrinsic cognitive load" of a complex lesson by establishing mental models of individual components before the learner is required to understand their systemic interactions.

The **Pre-training Principle** is a high-impact strategy for managing intrinsic cognitive load. It ensures that learners are not forced to identify individual components at the same time they are trying to understand complex causal relationships. By establishing "component models" (names and behaviors) first, you reserve the learner's limited working memory for building the "causal model" (the whole system) later.

## Design Guidelines

### PRE-G1. Foundational Vocabulary and Concept Identification
- **Priority:** High (Foundational)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Pre-Training Principle
- **Definition:** Providing a "glossary," introductory module, or "pre-lesson" that identifies the names and visual characteristics of main concepts before the primary lesson begins.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Teach the names and characteristics of main concepts before showing the full complex system. Build a foundation before introducing the whole process.
- **Rationale:** Complex systems have a cascading structure. Equipping learners with the prior knowledge needed to identify building blocks makes the whole system significantly easier to grasp, as they can dedicate their effort to processing interactions rather than identifying terms.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This strategy is essential for **Novice Learners**. For high-experience learners who already possess established mental models of the terms, this step provides no performance benefit and may even hinder them (Expertise Reversal Effect).
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Learning the four spheres of the Earth (atmosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere, geosphere) as standalone definitions before learning how they interact in a weather system.
    - Showing illustrations of major geological features like ridges or trenches before starting a simulation game.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Diving straight into a complex chemical reaction without explaining the individual elements involved.
    - An electrical engineering course where all phases focus immediately on how all components work together within a system.

### PRE-G2. Component Behavior Modeling
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Mental Model Construction / Pre-Training Principle
- **Definition:** Explaining how each part of a system works or moves individually before demonstrating how they all interact in the complex whole.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Establish "component models" (understanding names and specific behaviors) before building "causal models."
- **Rationale:** When a learner understands that a component (like a piston) can move forward or backward independently, they have the cognitive capacity left to understand *why* it moves in response to a systemic trigger later.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Only applies to systems where individual parts have unique, non-obvious states. If the behavior of a part is already intuitive or simple, defining it in isolation may create unnecessary redundancy.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Learning specifically that a master cylinder piston can move forward or backward before seeing it in a functional animation of a braking system.
    - An initial training phase in a safety test course focusing exclusively on how each individual electrical component works.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Presenting a causal chain—such as how stepping on a pedal moves a piston—to students who do not yet understand the individual parts' potential states.
    - A geology simulation game that expects learners to determine features like trenches without prior instruction on those features' specific behavioral indicators.

### PRE-G3. Cognitive Decoupling (Reducing "On-the-Fly" Learning)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Task Management
- **Definition:** Strategically separating the identification phase (what it is) from the application phase (how it functions) to ensure the learner isn't multitasking between the two.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Ensure the learner isn't overwhelmed by new parts and interactions at once by decoupling identification from system logic.
- **Rationale:** Pre-training minimizes the amount of processing needed during the actual lesson. If a component is "old news" to the brain, it can dedicate 100% of its resources to the new "system logic."
- **Boundary Conditions:** This decoupling is most critical in system-paced (automated) videos. If a video is learner-paced (pause/rewind), the need is slightly mitigated, though still recommended.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Using a physical plastic model to practice pulling and pushing a handle before viewing a narrated animation of a tire pump.
    - Inspecting a self-paced diagram of a pulley system before engaging with the main, fast-paced instructional text.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Presenting mathematics information concurrently with spreadsheet instruction for students unfamiliar with spreadsheets.
    - Providing essential supporting information during a troubleshooting simulation instead of beforehand.

### PRE-G4. Strategic Learner Calibration
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Individual Differences / Expertise Reversal
- **Definition:** The deliberate application of pre-training materials only to those learners who lack initial domain skills or prior knowledge.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Prioritize this principle specifically for learners who are completely new to the topic.
- **Rationale:** High-experience learners already possess mental models. Mandatory pre-training for experts is a waste of instructional time and can cause a decline in engagement due to the Expertise Reversal Effect.
- **Boundary Conditions:** In a "one-size-fits-all" video, pre-training should be made optional or skippable to accommodate mixed-ability classrooms.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Offering spreadsheet skill pre-training specifically to high school students with low initial skills.
    - Providing component-based pre-training to students with low levels of prior knowledge in electrical engineering.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Requiring mandatory pre-training on component names for high-knowledge apprentices in electrical systems.
    - Forcing pre-training in spreadsheet skills for high-knowledge graduate students.

# Personalization Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** PRZ

**Core Definition:** People learn more deeply from a multimedia presentation when the words are in a conversational style rather than a formal style. This principle is grounded in **Social Agency Theory**, which posits that when learners perceive a social partnership with the instructor or pedagogical agent, they exert more cognitive effort to make sense of the material.

## Design Guidelines

### PRZ-G1. Use Conversational Style (First and Second Person)
- **Priority:** High (Foundational)
- **Category:** Social Agency / Personalization Principle
- **Definition:** Replacing formal, third-person text or narration with a conversational style that utilizes first-person (*I*, *me*, *my*) and second-person (*you*, *your*) pronouns.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use personal possessives (e.g., "your lungs") instead of generic articles (e.g., "the lungs") and avoid referring to the student as "the learner" or "the user."
- **Rationale:** Conversational cues serve as social signals that prime the learner to view the interaction as a social partnership. This increases their motivational commitment to engage in active cognitive processing—selecting, organizing, and integrating information.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This strategy is most effective for learners who are not already highly motivated or high-achieving. The effect may diminish or become distracting in purely technical lessons exceeding 35 minutes.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - "I am going to tell you what happens when you breathe" instead of "This section describes the breathing process."
    - Asking "Did you know...?" instead of stating "It is a fact that..."
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A formal description: "The cloudy top extends into the freezing level."
    - Impersonal instructions: "The learner should now click the 'Next' button to continue the module."

### PRZ-G2. Incorporate Direct Address and Self-Revealing Comments
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Social Agency / Personalization Principle
- **Definition:** Including direct comments from the instructor to the learner, or brief self-revealing remarks that establish a human-like persona.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Add brief remarks that acknowledge the instructor’s presence or shared experience to build a sense of "social presence."
- **Rationale:** When learners feel a personal relationship with the instructor, they are more likely to exert the effort required for generative processing—deeply understanding the material rather than just memorizing it.
- **Boundary Conditions:** While establishing a persona is helpful, over-personalization in technical certification courses for experts may be perceived as unprofessional. It is most effective for novice learners.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An instructor adding: "I used to find this concept tricky, too, but let's look at it this way..."
    - A pedagogical agent saying: "Your mission is to learn how I can survive in different environments."
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A neutral instructional voice that provides information without ever acknowledging the learner's presence or the instructor's role.

### PRZ-G3. Use Polite Wording in Feedback and Instructions
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Social Agency / Politeness Principle
- **Definition:** Using cooperative phrasing (e.g., "Let's...", "You might want to...") instead of direct, authoritative, or impersonal commands, especially during feedback or hints.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Frame instructions as suggestions or collaborative efforts rather than "robotic" orders.
- **Rationale:** Politeness serves as a social cue that fosters a cooperative social partnership. Research indicates that learners process advice and feedback more deeply when it is delivered politely rather than authoritatively.
- **Boundary Conditions:** High-achieving, self-motivated students may process information deeply regardless of the social tone and may find excessive politeness unnecessary.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Providing a hint: "Shall we calculate the result now?" or "You might want to try selecting a different variable."
    - Using "Let's" to invite the student into the task: "Let's see what happens to the pressure when we move the piston."
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Direct, impersonal feedback: "No. Molecular weight is not part of this problem. Select another reason."
    - Authoritative commands: "Calculate the result now" or "Press the 'Enter' key."

### PRZ-G4. Authentic Socio-emotional Modeling (Refinement)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Social Agency / Video Engagement
- **Definition:** Sharing pedagogical vulnerabilities or growth-mindset cues to humanize the instructor and lower the learner's anxiety.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Acknowledge when a concept is traditionally difficult or share a brief, relevant anecdote about a personal learning struggle.
- **Rationale:** This deepens the "Social Partnership" beyond just conversational pronouns. It fosters trust and encourages learners to persevere through complex content by modeling that difficulty is part of the process.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Anecdotal content must be extremely brief (less than 15 seconds) to avoid violating the **Coherence Principle** (seductive details).
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - "I remember struggling with this formula when I first learned it, but here is the trick that helped me..."
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A 2-minute personal story that is unrelated to the learning objective.

# Voice Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** VOC

**Core Definition:** People learn more deeply when the words in a multimedia message are spoken by a friendly, human voice rather than a machine-like voice. Like the Personalization Principle, this is grounded in **Social Agency Theory**: a human voice serves as a social cue that primes the learner to engage in active, "sense-making" processing.

## Design Guidelines

### VOC-G1. Human vs. High-Fidelity Synthetic Voice Selection
- **Priority:** High (Foundational)
- **Category:** Social Agency / Voice Principle
- **Definition:** Utilizing a recorded human voice for narration rather than computer-generated speech that lacks natural prosody, rhythm, or inflection.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Always prioritize authentic human recordings. If a human narrator is unavailable, use only high-fidelity "Neural" TTS (Text-to-Speech) engines that possess human-like pitch and cadence.
- **Rationale:** Robotic or low-quality machine voices "depress" engagement because they feel unnatural and fail to activate the social agency response required for deep learning. If the voice sounds "inhuman," the learner invests less effort in cognitive sense-making.
- **Boundary Conditions:** The "Voice Effect" is most pronounced in longer lessons (>15 minutes) where the "social bond" is necessary to sustain attention. For very short, utilitarian instructions (e.g., "Click here"), a synthetic voice is acceptable. The benefit of a human voice may be significantly diminished or even eliminated if the on-screen pedagogical agent displaying the voice has **low embodiment** (i.e., it stands perfectly still while talking).
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A human instructor narrating a biology animation with natural pauses and warmth.
    - Using a high-quality, clear human recording with natural inflection to explain a math problem.
    - Using a high-end "Neural" AI voice that successfully mimics human pitch and cadence for a 5-minute tutorial.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Using a flat, robotic, and indifferent tone of voice for an instructional script.
    - Using a monotone, "robotic" TTS voice from the early 2000s to deliver a complex 20-minute lecture.
    - A voice with "glitchy" transitions or unnatural pronunciations that constantly remind the learner they are listening to a machine.
    - Using a text-to-speech engine that mispronounces technical terms or uses incorrect emphasis.

### VOC-G2. Speaker Enthusiasm and Energy
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Social Agency / Video Engagement
- **Definition:** Delivering narration with a high degree of energy, varied intonation, and a "teaching" persona rather than a "reading" persona.
- **Rule of Thumb:** The narrator should sound genuinely interested in the topic; avoid a disinterested, monotone lecture delivery.
- **Rationale:** Speaker enthusiasm is a primary driver of student engagement. An energetic voice acts as a powerful social cue that motivates the learner to stay focused, especially as video length increases.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Enthusiasm must remain authentic. "Artificial" or overly manic energy can become a **Seductive Detail** that distracts from the content.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An instructor using pitch changes to emphasize a "surprising" data point in a chart.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - An instructor reading a script in a flat, tired, or bored tone that sounds like a disinterested recitation.
    - Ensuring a professional narrator uses clear prosody (natural rhythm and emphasis) rather than a monotone delivery.

### VOC-G3. Use Standard-Accented Voice for Narration
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Social Agency / Cognitive Load
- **Definition:** Ensuring the human narrator speaks with a standard accent that is familiar, clear, and expected by the target audience.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Avoid voices that the learner may find perceptually different or difficult to process, such as heavy foreign accents, unless contextually required.
- **Rationale:** A standard accent is a positive social cue that supports the sense of a social partnership. Conversely, a non-standard or heavy accent can increase the learner's **cognitive load** as they must exert extra effort to decipher the words, which leaves less mental capacity for learning the actual content.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This does not apply where a diverse range of accents is the subject of study (e.g., language learning) or where the "social presence" of a specific persona requires a specific dialect. If the speaker and the audience share the same non-standard accent (e.g., russian accent), the accent will not cause much extraneous cognitive load.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Narrating an English-language lesson on physics using a native speaker with a clear, standard accent familiar to the students.
    - Narrating a cultural immersion lesson for new Hispanic immigrants using a human voice in English with a corresponding familiar accent (Spanish accent).
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Narrating a lightning lesson for English speakers using a human voice with a heavy, non-standard Russian accent.
    - A human recording that is so low-quality or poorly articulated that it mimics the lack of natural prosody found in machines.

### VOC-G4. Coordinate Voice with High Embodiment
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Social Agency / Personalization Principle
- **Definition:** When using an on-screen pedagogical agent or instructor, the human voice must be paired with human-like "embodiment cues" such as gestures, facial expressions, and movement.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Never pair a human voice with a completely static or robotic on-screen character.
- **Rationale:** If an agent has a human voice but remains static, the conflicting social cues (human sound vs. robotic appearance) can create a "mismatch" that diminishes the learner’s sense of social agency and lowers engagement.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This guideline only applies when a visual agent (character or instructor) is present on the screen. It does not apply to "voice-over only" narration where no character is shown.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An on-screen agent using a human voice while simultaneously pointing to relevant features on a diagram and making eye contact with the learner.
    - A character that nods or changes facial expressions to emphasize key points in the narration.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - An agent that uses a human voice but stands completely motionless on the screen like a cardboard cutout.
    - A character whose lips do not move or whose gestures are entirely out of sync with the human speech.

### VOC-G5. Emotional Prosody and Inflection
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Social Agency
- **Definition:** Using vocal inflection (prosody) to signal importance and guide the learner's understanding of the material's structure.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use a "downward" inflection for conclusions and an "upward" inflection or increased pitch for key terms or questions.
- **Rationale:** Prosody acts as "auditory signaling." It tells the learner's brain which words are the most important, reducing the cognitive effort required to organize the incoming verbal stream.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Excessive prosody (sounding like a children's show host) can be perceived as condescending by adult learners or experts.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Pausing for 0.5 seconds and slightly raising the pitch when introducing a new technical term.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A monotone delivery where the importance of every word sounds identical, providing no structural cues to the learner.

# Image Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** IMG

**Core Definition:** The mere presence of an instructor’s image on the screen does not necessarily enhance learning. When an image is static, non-interactive, or poorly framed, it acts as a "seductive detail" that consumes limited cognitive resources without aiding understanding. Social Agency is only activated when the image serves a functional or interpersonal purpose. This is supported by a median effect size of d=0.20, suggesting that adding a face just for the sake of it is rarely worth the cognitive cost.

## Design Guidelines

### IMG-G1. Strategic Timing for Social Presence
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Social Agency / Cognitive Load
- **Definition:** Incorporate the speaker’s on-screen appearance during "Social Priming" phases: the introduction, transitions, and the conclusion, as well as during occasional low-stimulus phases: no other essential or complex visuals are present at the same time.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use the speaker's video to initiate the partnership at the start, then "fade out" during the complex segments of the lesson, and reintroduce it when there’s not much visual action taking place on the screen.
- **Rationale:** Social cues are most effective during the initial "priming" phase. Once the partnership is established, the face is no longer needed to maintain motivation and can be removed to free up the visual channel for heavy cognitive tasks. When there’s not much visual stimulus on the screen, the talking head can help maintain or boost learner engagement.
- **Boundary Conditions:** In highly technical lessons, if the initial visual load is already extreme, even an introductory video may be better replaced by a voice-over.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An instructor appears on camera to explain the "why" behind a module, then switches to a full-screen screen-capture for the step-by-step tutorial.
    - In mid-phases, no new visual contents are introduced on the screen. Instead of using narrations over the previous visual content on the screen, an instructor talking video is brought back to the screen as it is better than showing the irrelevant old visuals.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - No instructor talking-head or talking avatar is added to the video even though the video is long enough (3-minute long) to benefit from it.
    - Keeping a large video of the presenter on-screen throughout a 60-minute technical software tutorial.

### IMG-G2. Avoid Non-Functional Talking-Head Video Window (Picture-in-Picture)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Seductive Details
- **Definition:** Hide the instructor's talking head video window (picture-in-picture webcam video) when complex visual aids (diagrams, charts, or screencasts) are the primary focus.
- **Rule of Thumb:** The face should be visible only when it is the *primary* source of information; it should disappear the moment a complex graphic is introduced.
- **Rationale:** An on-screen face in the picture-in-picture format doesn’t interact with the slide content very well and acts as a "seductive detail." Because faces are high-salience stimuli, they create "split-attention" when paired with complex diagrams, forcing the brain to choose between processing the instructor's expressions or the instructional data.
- **Boundary Conditions:** The instructor's face in the picture-in-picture format should remain on screen during introductions, conclusions, or thematic transitions where no other complex visual information is presented.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - The video transitions to a full-screen view of a chemical reaction diagram the moment the instructor begins describing the molecular bonding process.
    - An instructor talking head is present when simple graphics with low element interactivity is displayed on the screen.
    - An instructor talking head is present when the instructor is just talking without introducing new visuals (words, graphics).
    - An instructor talking head is present when only simple visuals (bullet points) are presented on the screen.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - No instructor or avatar video is used anywhere in the video even though the video is long enough (3-minute long) to benefit from it.
    - A webcam window takes up significant screen real estate while the audio describes a fast-moving, high-detail animation of a solar cell.

### IMG-G3. Optimal Visual Framing (Medium vs. Long Shots)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Social Agency / Video Engagement
- **Definition:** Add the instructor video by framing the instructor in a "Medium Shot" (waist/chest up) rather than a "Long Shot" (full body/distant) to maximize social presence.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Ensure the instructor’s face and upper-body gestures are clearly visible and occupy a significant portion of their dedicated video window.
- **Rationale:** Medium shots simulate a natural social distance, triggering the "Social Agency" response where learners perceive a social partnership. Long shots or distant "lectern shots" create a sense of detachment and lower engagement, especially in videos longer than 6 minutes.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Use a long shot only if the instructional goal requires seeing the full body (e.g., a physical demonstration or dance step).
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An instructor framed from the chest up during a 12-minute lecture, maintaining 70% retention.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - No instructor or avatar video is used anywhere in the video even though the video is long enough (3-minute long) to benefit from it.
    - Filming a teacher at the front of a large lecture hall where they appear small and distant (Long Shot), leading to a predicted engagement drop to 20%.

### IMG-G4. High Embodiment and Attention Guidance
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Social Agency / Signaling
- **Definition:** Ensuring that if an instructor or instructor’s talking avatar is visible, they utilize human-like nonverbal communication, specifically pointing and eye gaze to direct the learner’s attention as well as hand and body movements to demonstrate behaviors, actions, and events.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Visible characters must actively guide the learner's attention to the important parts of the screen content through pointing gestures and gaze shifting or communicate messages like behaviors, actions, and events through nonverbal gestures.
- **Rationale:** Pointing and gaze shifting act as cognitive aids. They synchronize the visual and auditory channels by showing the learner exactly where to look at the moment a concept is discussed, reducing "visual search" load. And certain concepts, behaviors, actions, and events can be presented through nonverbal gestures more effectively than using pictures and words.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Pair with a human voice (Voice Principle). A gesturing character paired with a robotic voice creates a "social cue mismatch" that can be distracting.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A talking avatar looking at and pointing to specific parts of a circuit diagram as they are described.
    - An instructor talking head is present when the instructor uses pointing gestures to point to relevant parts of the screen.
    - An instructor talking head is present when the instructor uses explanatory gestures to illustrate the concept, motions, or body movements.
    - An instructor video is integrated with the slide (the instructor video’s background is removed) and the instructor points at exact parts of the screen content as the instructor talks about them.
    - An instructor video is not fully integrated with the screen content (picture-in-picture) but uses hand gestures to illustrate the key concepts such as “embrace,” “love,” “high/low,” and “big/small.”
    - An instructor video is not fully integrated with the screen content (picture-in-picture) but uses hand gestures and body movements to demonstrate a breathing technique when discussing stress-relief techniques.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - No instructor or avatar video is used anywhere in the video even though the video is long enough (3-minute long) to benefit from it.
    - A "talking head" video where the instructor looks directly into the lens (audience) while describing a chart to their left without ever acknowledging it visually or physically.
    - A small “talking head” video that only shows the instructor face in the picture-in-picture format is too small and limiting to guide the learner’s attention or present emotional messages.

### IMG-G5. Non-Functional Static Imagery vs. Talking-Head Video
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Cognitive Load Management)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Image Principle
- **Definition:** Add the recording of the instructor’s talking head or an animated talking avatar. Avoid the use of static headshots, motionless avatars, or "frozen" instructor photos that do not interact with the instructional content.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use dynamic talking head videos. Do not use static speaker images as they serve a very little purpose.
- **Rationale:** Humans are biologically hardwired to attend to faces. A non-functional, static image of a speaker competes for the learner's limited visual attention without providing any instructional benefit, leading to extraneous cognitive load.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Static images may be acceptable in "visual-lean" environments (text-only) to provide a minimal social cue for motivation, provided they do not obscure content.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A science lesson where the learner hears the instructor’s voice while viewing complex diagrams, with no image or video of the instructor visible.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - No instructor talking head or avatar videos are used anywhere in the video even though the video is long enough (3-minute long) to benefit from it.
    - Adding a small photograph or cartoon avatar of the lecturer to the bottom corner of every slide in a technical presentation.

# Embodiment Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** EMB

**Core Definition:** People learn more deeply when on-screen agents or instructors display human-like behaviors such as gesturing, movement, eye contact, and facial expressions. This principle transforms a "talking head" from a passive image into a **Social Agent** that primes the learner for active generative processing.

This principle is rooted in **Social Agency Theory**, which suggests that nonverbal cues serve as powerful social signals. These signals prime the learner to perceive the computer as a social partner, increasing their motivational commitment to engage in the deep "generative processing" (selecting, organizing, and integrating) required for learning.

## Design Guidelines

### EMB-G1. Deictic (Pointing) and Iconic Gesturing
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Attention Guidance)
- **Category:** Attention Guidance / Signaling
- **Definition:** The use of hand gestures to physically point to content (deictic) or mimic the shape/action of a concept (iconic).
- **Rule of Thumb:** If an instructor is on screen, they must use their hands to "anchor" the verbal message to the visual aids.
- **Rationale:** Gestures serve as a form of "embodied signaling." They reduce the learner's visual search time and provide a secondary visual cue that reinforces the verbal explanation, making the interaction feel authentic and instructionally purposeful.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Movements must be synchronized. Random or "looping" idle animations (like a character just swaying) can become **Seductive Details** that increase extraneous load.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An onscreen character named **Dr. Phyz** who "flies" toward and circles relevant portions of a circuit diagram during a complex physics explanation.
    - An instructor using a "sweeping" hand gesture to indicate the direction of blood flow in a heart diagram.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A digital tutor who stands with hands behind their back while describing a multi-step process shown on a slide.
    - An onscreen character that only moves its lips while the rest of its body remains frozen.

### EMB-G2. Gaze Shifting and Joint Attention
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Social Agency / Attention Guidance
- **Definition:** Coordinating the instructor’s or avatar’s eye gaze and head orientation to shift between the learner (the camera) and the instructional content.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use "Eye Contact" to build rapport during introductions and "Joint Attention" (looking at the diagram) during the explanation.
- **Rationale:** Humans are socially primed to follow the gaze of others. When an instructor looks at a diagram, the learner's eyes instinctively follow, facilitating the integration of the verbal and visual channels.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Avoid the **"Uncanny Valley"**—if an agent’s gaze is inconsistent or "dead-eyed" (looking away while speaking to the learner), it creates a social mismatch that is unnerving and distracting.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An instructor’s head orientation shifts toward a solar cell diagram at the exact moment they begin to describe how the cells work.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A character that looks fixedly into the camera lens even while describing a complex graphic located at the far edge of the screen.

### EMB-G3. Phonemic Lip-Sync Coordination
- **Priority:** Medium (Crucial for Realism)
- **Category:** Social Agency / Cognitive Load
- **Definition:** The precise coordination of the mouth and lip movements with the timing and phonemes of the spoken narration.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Synchronize auditory and visual channels perfectly to maintain the "Social Partner" illusion.
- **Rationale:** Discrepancies between what is heard and what is seen (lip-sync errors) create a cognitive conflict. The learner wastes resources trying to "fix" the mismatch rather than processing the lesson content.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Stylized, non-human icons (like a simple robot) have a higher tolerance for simplified "mouth-open/mouth-closed" animation. 3D human models require high precision.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A 3D agent whose mouth shapes accurately reflect the vowels and consonants being spoken in a high-speed technical tutorial.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - An agent whose lip movements continue for several seconds after the audio narration has stopped.

### EMB-G4. Modality Congruence (Voice-Body Match)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Social Agency / Voice Principle
- **Definition:** The strategic pairing of high-embodiment behaviors (gestures/movement) with a standard-accented human voice.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Never pair a highly expressive, gesturing agent with a machine-synthesized (robotic) voice.
- **Rationale:** The "Embodiment Effect" relies on the learner’s perception of a social entity. If an agent looks human (gesturing) but sounds robotic, the conflicting cues create a "social cue mismatch" that nullifies the learning benefit.
- **Boundary Conditions:** If a human voice is unavailable, it is better to use a non-human icon (like a simple robot) than a highly animated agent with a robotic voice.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A gesturing instructor speaking in a warm, energetic human voice with natural prosody.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A highly animated, expressive agent using a flat, robotic TTS voice.

### EMB-G5. Postural Shifting and Social Presence
- **Priority:** Low
- **Category:** Social Agency / Video Engagement
- **Definition:** Subtle, non-deictic movements such as nodding, shifting weight, or changing posture to maintain a sense of "life" in the avatar or instructor.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use subtle postural shifts during transitions or when the instructor is the primary focus of the screen.
- **Rationale:** These movements increase "social presence," making the instructor feel like a living being rather than a recorded artifact. This sustained social bond keeps the learner motivated during longer segments.
- **Boundary Conditions:** These movements must be minimized during the "heavy lifting" of a lesson to avoid becoming a distraction from the complex visuals with high-element interactivity.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - In an interactive video requiring student input, an instructor nodded in agreement with a correct answer provided by the learner during an interactive check-point.
    - A digital tutor naturally shifts its weight from one foot to another during an introductory "hook."

### EMB-G6. Strategic Use of Talking Head
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Social Agency / Video Engagement
- **Definition:** Speaker’s talking head video is added to the video in ways that do not interfere with other essential visuals in the video.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Add the speaker’s talking head video when the instructor is the primary focus of the screen, when there’s no high element interactivity on the screen, when the video does not provide enough stimuli without the talking head, or when there’s nothing new to show visually.
- **Rationale:** Talking head video increases "social presence," motivating students to make more effort for cognitive processing. This sustained social bond keeps the learner motivated during longer segments.
- **Boundary Conditions:** The talking head must be removed during the "heavy lifting" of a lesson to avoid becoming a distraction from the essential visuals on the screen (e.g., complex diagrams).
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An instructor talking head is present when the instructor is just talking without introducing new visuals (words, graphics).
    - An instructor talking head is present when only simple visuals (bullet points) are presented on the screen.
    - An instructor talking head is present when simple graphics with low element interactivity is displayed on the screen.
    - An instructor talking head is present when the instructor uses pointing gestures to point to relevant parts of the screen.
    - An instructor talking head is present when the instructor uses explanatory gestures to illustrate the concept, motions, or body movements.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - An instructor taking head is present when a diagram with high element interactivity is presented and the instructor does not use any pointing or explanatory gestures.

# Animation Principles Framework

## Principle 1: Define a Clear Instructional Purpose

**Master ID Prefix:** AN1

**Core Definition:** People learn better from an animation when its instructional purpose is explicitly defined. This prevents "decorative motion" and ensures that the animation's complexity scales with the intended learning outcome (Structure vs. Process).

## Design Guidelines

### AN1-G1. Objective Specification (Structure vs. Process)
- **Priority:** High (Foundational for AI Logic)
- **Category:** Instructional Design / Animation Principle
- **Definition:** Explicitly state the purpose or the focus point of an animation—whether the animation is intended to introduce the **static structure** (where things are) or explain the **dynamic process** (how they interact).
- **Rule of Thumb:** Align the animation type to the verb in the learning objective (e.g., "Identify" = Structure; "Explain" = Process).
- **Rationale:** Due to the transient, fluid, and complex nature of an animation, structure and process require different cognitive "mapping." Without a defined goal, learners may attend to complex motion (process) when they should be focusing on spatial orientation (structure), leading to cognitive interference.
- **Boundary Conditions:** For extremely simple systems (e.g., a basic lever), structure and function are often processed simultaneously, making this distinction less critical.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A module stating: "This animation identifies the four chambers of the heart and their spatial orientation."
    - An objective that separates "identifying components" from "understanding the sequence of ignition."
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Using a high-speed, moving animation when the learner only needs to memorize the names of static parts.
    - Including an animation primarily for "affective" benefits (making the lesson look "high-tech") without a pedagogical goal.

### AN1-G2. Build Animation (Structural Strategy)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Spatial Scaffolding / Animation Principle
- **Definition:** If describing a device’s full structure, progressively introduce parts of a system one-by-one to "build" the device's full structure on screen.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use a "build" animation to teach names, spatial relationships, and static orientation to novices.
- **Rationale:** Serial presentation acts as a natural "segmenter." It prevents the learner from being overwhelmed by a fully assembled complex device and ensures they form a mental model of each individual part before seeing the whole.
- **Boundary Conditions:** High-knowledge learners may find "build" animations too slow, potentially triggering the **Redundancy Effect**.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A "build animation" that introduces the piano key, then the lever, then the hammer to teach assembly.
    - An architectural animation showing a skeletal frame erected before walls are added.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Showing a fully assembled, complex engine immediately to a novice who cannot distinguish the individual parts.
    - Using a fast-moving "process" animation when the goal is for learners to simply identify the spatial locations of parts.

### AN1-G3. Process Animation (Functional Strategy)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Causal Reasoning / Animation Principle
- **Definition:** If describing how parts of a system behave, move, and interact with one another over time, use an animation to portray it.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use a **process animation** only when the goal is to explain **causal relationships** or spatiotemporal interactions.
- **Rationale:** Process animations explicitly represent behaviors that are difficult to mentally animate (e.g., fluid dynamics). They help learners transition from "what it is" to "how it works." Well-designed process animations provide a clear portrayal of interaction (e.g., parts moving on pivots) and help learners appreciate the complex relationships central to functionality.
- **Boundary Conditions:** These can easily cause cognitive overload if they are too fast. They often **must** be supported by the **Signaling** and **Segmenting** principles.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An animation showing how piano parts interact to strike a string and then reset.
    - A fluid dynamics animation showing air moving over a wing to create lift.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Using a realistic process animation that is so visually "busy" that the learner cannot isolate the behavior of a single part.
    - Implementing a "process animation" when the intended goal is only for learners to identify the names of parts.

### AN1-G4. Dynamic Highlighting (Cues)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Attention Guidance / Signaling
- **Definition:** If the viewer needs to focus on the specific parts of an animation, use “dynamic highlighting” (cues) to guide the viewer's eye.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use "dynamic highlighting" (cues) to ensure the learner's gaze is in the right place during "fluent" motion.
- **Rationale:** Because animations are continuous, learners often miss vital but subtle movements. The "Directing" function (cues) ensures that the motion doesn't move faster than the learner's attention.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Vital for novices; less necessary for experts who have developed internal "schemata" to find relevant cues naturally.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Using a glowing border to focus attention on a small, moving part within a larger machine.
    - A narrator saying "Watch the blue lever" while that specific part flashes.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Relying solely on the movement of a part to "catch" the eye, which often fails in complex displays.
    - Using animations primarily for "affective considerations," such as marketing appeal, which obscures the actual instructional message.

## Principle 2: Balance Spatial and Temporal Information

**Master ID Prefix:** AN2

**Core Definition:** People learn more deeply from an animation when appropriate emphasis is given to **spatial** (details of form) versus **temporal** (flow of change) information. This balance ensures that the "fluency" of motion does not mask the critical details needed for fine-grained analysis.

## Design Guidelines

### AN2-G1. Static vs. Dynamic Selection
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Cognitive Economy)
- **Category:** Media Format / Animation Principle
- **Definition:** If describing a complex process, animate it via a moving graphic or a series of static images based on the difficulty of inferring temporal change.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use animation **only** when overall dynamics are essential and temporal inferences are too complex to make from static depictions.
- **Rationale:** Static graphics are often superior for fine-grained analysis because they allow learners to interrogate specific states more closely. Animation is "fluent"—it can mask subtle spatial configurations because the eye is too busy tracking the motion.
- **Boundary Conditions:** High-knowledge learners (experts) can often "mentally animate" static parts, making motion redundant for them.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Using an animation for a complex piano mechanism to explicitly show how parts interact on pivots.
    - Using static images of successive stages (e.g., a kangaroo hop) to allow learners to interrogate subtle bodily configurations.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Using a continuous, high-speed animation for a task that requires a detailed analysis of fleeting configurations (e.g., gait analysis), where the core data is lost in the "flow."

### AN2-G2. Temporal Zooming (Perceptual Threshold Manipulation)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Temporal Processing / Animation Principle
- **Definition:** If describing a process that is difficult to perceive because it’s too slow or too fast for the human eye to track, use slow motion or time-lapse animations to adjust the speed of a process and bring it into the range of human sensory sensitivity (human perceptual threshold).
- **Rule of Thumb:** If the real-life process is too fast or too slow for the human eye to track, you **must** use temporal zooming to trigger the brain's dynamic perceptual schemata.
- **Rationale:** Processes like plant growth (too slow) or a chameleon strike (too fast) lie outside the human "perceptual window." Manipulation makes the "change" perceptible and therefore learnable.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Clearly label the playback speed (e.g., "0.25x speed") so learners do not form misconceptions about the real-world velocity of the process.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Using "temporal zooming out" (time-lapse) to speed up plant growth so the movement becomes perceptible.
    - Using "temporal zooming in" (slow motion) to slow down a chameleon’s tongue strike.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Showing a high-speed chemical reaction at real-life speed, where the core interaction is finished before the learner can even register its presence.

### AN2-G3. Hybrid (Static-Dynamic) Displays
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Presentation Strategy
- **Definition:** If describing the “Big Picture” of motion and the “Micro-Details” of specific configurations, combine animations with complementary static snapshots to facilitate the analysis of specific states within a continuous sequence.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use animations for the **"Big Picture"** of motion and static snapshots for the **"Micro-Details"** of specific configurations.
- **Rationale:** Animations are "transient"—the information disappears as it moves. Providing a simultaneous "filmstrip" of key frames allows the learner to repeatedly interrogate and compare different states without relying on memory.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This is less necessary for very simple, linear animations where there are no "micro-level" changes to miss.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Providing a video of a solar flare next to three static images highlighting the flare's beginning, peak, and end.
    - Using a "filmstrip" view of key frames below a main animation window to allow the learner to compare states side-by-side.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Relying solely on a one-time animation to teach a process where specific, subtle "states" are the primary learning objective.

## Principle 3: Align Perceptual Attributes with Cognitive Requirements

**Master ID Prefix:** AN3

**Core Definition:** People learn more deeply when the perceptual attributes (what is visually prominent) are aligned with the cognitive requirements (what is instructionally important). This principle ensures that the learner's biological "attention capture" is synchronized with the pedagogical goals of the lesson.

### AN3-G1. Salience Management (Salience-Relevance Alignment)
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Attention Guidance)
- **Category:** Attention Guidance / Animation Principle
- **Definition:** When describing a system, process, or structure, animate it by emphasizing the thematically important parts of an animation so that they are the most perceptually noticeable (conspicuous) elements on the screen.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Align "Visual Weight"—brightness, motion, speed, or contrast—with instructional importance so that subtle but vital information is not ignored.
- **Rationale:** Human perception is biologically biased toward salient features (the largest, fastest, or brightest objects). If irrelevant parts are highly salient, they consume limited cognitive resources ("attention capture"), leaving the learner unable to process the subtle, functionally relevant parts.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This is less critical for experts who have developed internal schemata to filter out "visual noise." In rare cases, such as camouflage detection or anomaly training, misalignment may be a deliberate learning goal.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Speeding up an animation of a pendulum clock (e.g., 200x speed) to make the descending weight—usually too slow to be noticed—perceptually salient.
    - Using "Temporal Zooming" (slow motion) to make a chameleon's high-speed tongue strike visible and salient.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A "veridical" (realistic) animation of a piano mechanism where a large, sweeping hammer (salient) masks the behavior of the tiny, functionally crucial "jack" (relevant).
    - A lightning animation where the bright flash (salient) is so overwhelming that the learner cannot see the "stepped leaders" forming the path.

### AN3-G2. Strategic Abstraction (Logic over Realism)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Animation Principle
- **Definition:** When describing a complex matter with rich details and high element interactivity, animate it by deliberately simplifying or altering its literal appearance to prioritize core conceptual clarity over "behavioral realism."
- **Rule of Thumb:** **Clarity > Realism.** Do not be restricted by how a subject *actually* looks if a simplified version makes the mechanism easier to understand.
- **Rationale:** High realism often introduces "visual noise" (shadows, textures, complex reflections) that distracts from the underlying logic. Abstraction focuses the learner’s limited processing capacity on essential causal relationships.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Realism is necessary for high-fidelity tasks where recognizing physical nuances is the goal (e.g., identifying specific tissue types in surgical training).
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Deviating from a heart's actual appearance to create a "schematic" 3D version that clearly highlights blood flow directions.
    - Using "Ghosted" or "Cutaway" views to remove an engine's outer casing, revealing the internal mechanical interactions.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Using a hyper-realistic 3D render of a cellular process where complex surface textures hide the internal functional changes of the organelles.

### AN3-G3. Hierarchical Extraction (Macro vs. Micro Management)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Perceptual Processing / Animation Principle
- **Definition:** When describing a complex system, animate it by designing animations to help learners process both the overall patterns (macro-level) and the fine-grained details (micro-level) of a system simultaneously.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Ensure the "Big Picture" motion does not mask the "Small Detail" configurations.
- **Rationale:** Simultaneous macro and micro structures pose major processing challenges. Coherent mental models require understanding how the *whole* system moves while also noticing how *individual* parts behave. Often, the macro motion is so salient it "blinds" the viewer to the micro details.
- **Boundary Conditions:** If a task is purely high-level (e.g., predicting weather direction), micro-details should be omitted entirely to avoid unnecessary cognitive load.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An animation of a kangaroo hop using "Picture-in-Picture" to show the whole body trajectory (macro) while zooming in on the tail's counter-balance movement (micro).
    - A world map animation showing global trade routes (macro) with "Magnifying Glass" hovers for individual shipping ports (micro).
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A kangaroo hop animation where the rapid hopping pattern (macro) makes it impossible to see the subtle configuration changes in the feet and tail (micro).
    - A factory assembly line animation where the conveyor belt speed prevents the learner from seeing the specific action of a single robotic arm.

## Principle 4: Support Perceptual and Cognitive Processing

**Master ID Prefix:** AN4

**Core Definition:** People learn more deeply from an animation when the acts of seeing (perceptual) and understanding (cognitive) are explicitly supported. This principle focuses on methods to prevent "motion blindness"—the phenomenon where constant movement prevents the brain from consolidating information.

### AN4-G1. Dynamic Signaling (Progressive Path Cueing)
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Complex Systems)
- **Category:** Attention Guidance / Animation Principle
- **Definition:** When describing different parts of a process or a dynamic action/behavior, animate it and also utilize high-contrast, moving visual cues to guide the learner's attention along causal chains and functional relationships.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use "Progressive Path" cues (moving ribbons of light/color) rather than static visuospatial cues (like arrows) to direct attention in a moving display.
- **Rationale:** Conventional static arrows often cannot compete with the powerful attention-directing effect of dynamic contrast in an animation. Progressive path cueing recruits that same dynamic contrast to ensure the learner's eye follows the most instructionally relevant path without getting "lost" in background motion.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Over-signaling can lead to "Guidance Seduction," where the learner follows the cue mechanically (like a cat chasing a laser pointer) without actually processing the underlying logic.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A moving "glow" that travels through a series of pipes to show the exact path of liquid in a hydraulic system.
    - Implementing moving ribbons of color to guide a learner along the entire causal chain of weather system formation.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Relying on simple, static arrow cues that are easily obscured by other moving parts in a complex 3D animation.

### AN4-G2. Directed Verbal Guidance (The "Tour Guide" Script)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Social Agency / Attention Guidance
- **Definition:** When showing an animation, utilize spoken narration to provide explicit instructions on **where**, **when**, and **how** to observe the animation.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Reconceptualize the narration script as a "Tour Guide" that directs the learner’s gaze and focus in real-time.
- **Rationale:** Because visual perception is highly selective, learners often attend to "high salience" (bright/fast) elements instead of "high relevance" (functional) elements. Spoken words act as a corrective force to fix these salience-relevance mismatches.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Highly effective for novices; however, for experts with high internal guidance, excessive verbal direction can become redundant.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A narrator stating: "Notice how the small valve at the bottom right opens only when the piston reaches its peak."
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A narrator describing the broad history of an engine while a complex animation of its internal components is running, providing zero guidance on which specific component to observe.

### AN4-G3. Intra-Representational Modality Alignment
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Modality Principle
- **Definition:** During an active animation, exclusively use the auditory channel (narration) instead of the visual channel (on-screen text) to provide guidance.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Favor spoken words over on-screen text labels or captions when explaining moving parts of an animation.
- **Rationale:** On-screen text forces the eye to jump between the text and the animation (**Split Attention**), which overloads the visual channel and causes the learner to miss fleeting movements. Auditory guidance allows the visual channel to focus 100% on the motion.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Technical terms with difficult spellings may require brief on-screen labels. This is also a critical exception for accessibility (Deaf/Hard of Hearing learners).
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Using a human voice to describe the movement of a chameleon's tongue so the eyes never have to leave the tongue to read a caption.
    - Pairing an animation of a heart beating with a spoken explanation of the valve cycle.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Presenting a complex animation with floating "text bubbles" that the learner must read while simultaneously trying to track the motion.
    - Placing a paragraph of explanatory text next to an animation, leading to "intra-representational split attention" where information is lost during the eye-switch.

## Principle 5: Tailor Interaction to Learner Expertise

**Master ID Prefix:** AN5

**Core Definition:** People learn more deeply from an animation when interaction opportunities accord with the instructional aims and the expertise of the learner. This principle addresses the "human element"—ensuring that the level of control and interactivity matches the learner's ability to process dynamic information.

### AN5-G1. Intuitive Navigation Controls (No-Search Interface)
- **Priority:** Low
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / UI Design
- **Definition:** Include simple, standard navigation features (play, pause, stop, and speed control) that allow learners to manage the information flow without learning a new interface.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Navigation must be "invisible." If a learner has to think about *how* to pause, they aren't thinking about *what* they are learning.
- **Rationale:** Navigation imposes **Extraneous Processing**. Every second spent on a "hide-and-seek" interface (where controls are hidden or non-standard) is a second of "cognitive leakage" away from the instructional content.
- **Boundary Conditions:** For extremely short, "looping" animations (under 5 seconds) illustrating a single movement, a full control bar may be unnecessary visual clutter.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Using a standard "Pause/Play" toggle consistently located in the same UI position for every video.
    - Enabling a "Step" function that allows learners to move frame-by-frame to analyze micro-level changes.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A custom video player where the volume and play buttons use non-standard icons that require a "hover" to identify.
    - A "minimalist" design where controls only appear when the mouse is in a very specific, non-obvious location.

### AN5-G2. Strategic Scaffolding (Constrained vs. Open Control)
- **Priority:** Low
- **Category:** Expertise Reversal / Learner Calibration
- **Definition:** When showing an animation, provide different levels of interactive freedom based on prior knowledge; specifically, guiding novices through a "safe" path while allowing experts total flexibility.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use "Lock-step" navigation for novices to ensure they don't miss foundational steps; provide "Open" navigation for experts.
- **Rationale:** Novices lack the **Schemata** to know what is important. Without constraints, they are often "captured" by high-salience but irrelevant details. Experts, conversely, know exactly what they need and find constraints frustrating and redundant (Expertise Reversal Effect).
- **Boundary Conditions:** Constraints must be meaningful. Locking a video just to "force" a view without a pedagogical check-point is a compliance tactic, not a learning strategy.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A novice module that "locks" the next chapter until the user has clicked on three specific "hotspots" to identify components.
    - Providing novices with a "Guided Tour" mode that auto-pauses at functionally crucial moments.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Granting a novice total freedom to scrub through a complex meteorological animation, leading them to miss subtle pressure changes while watching the "pretty" cloud movement.
    - Forcing a senior engineer to sit through a rigid, un-skippable "parts identification" segment of a system they have already mastered.

### AN5-G3. Iterative Interrogation (Temporal Pacing)
- **Priority:** Low
- **Category:** Perceptual Processing / Animation Principle
- **Definition:** When showing a “dense” or fast-paced animation, enable and prompt learners to view an animation multiple times at variable speeds to unpack different layers of the system.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Provide variable speed controls (0.5x, 1x, 2x) to change the **"Perceptibility Profile"** of the animation.
- **Rationale:** Complex animations contain both macro-level patterns (the system) and micro-level details (the parts). High speeds favor macro patterns; slow speeds favor micro-details. Iteration at different speeds allows the brain to "interrogate" both layers.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Most effective for "dense" animations. For very simple animations, providing multiple speeds may add unnecessary complexity to the interface.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An instructor prompt: "Now watch the assembly again at 0.5x speed to see how the spring catches the lever."
    - A "Slow-Mo" toggle specifically for a high-speed mechanical interaction that is usually a blur.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Forcing a learner to watch a 10-minute complex animation only once at a fixed speed before moving to an assessment.
    - Providing a "Seek" bar that is too sensitive, making it impossible to return to a specific 1-second window.

# Individual Differences Principle Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** IDD

**Core Definition:** Design principles that facilitate learning for low-knowledge or low-spatial learners may have a neutral or even negative effect on high-knowledge or high-spatial learners. This principle acknowledges that instructional "crutches" for beginners can become "clutter" for experts—a phenomenon known as the **Expertise Reversal Effect**.

### IDD-G1. Prior Knowledge Alignment (Expertise Reversal)
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Learner Calibration)
- **Category:** Learner Calibration / Expertise Reversal
- **Definition:** Adjust the level of instructional support, visual scaffolding, and detail based on the learner's existing mental models and domain experience.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Provide high levels of guidance (graphics, animations, segmenting, signaling, worked examples) for novices; "fade" or remove these supports for experts to avoid redundant processing.
- **Rationale:** Novices lack internal schemata and require external structure to organize information. Experts already possess these internal structures; forcing them to process external guidance that duplicates their internal knowledge creates **Redundancy**, which consumes working memory and inhibits learning.
- **Boundary Conditions:** In high-stakes/high-stress environments (e.g., emergency medicine or aviation), even experts benefit from redundant scaffolding (checklists) to prevent performance slips.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A software tutorial that offers a "Skip to Advanced" button or a pre-test to bypass introductory modules.
    - Providing a fully worked-out math problem for a beginner, but giving an expert only the final goal to solve independently.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A mandatory "How to use a mouse" module for a group of senior software engineers.
    - Forcing an expert chemist to watch a slow-paced animation of basic molecular bonding before accessing a complex simulation.

### IDD-G2. Cognitive Capacity Personalization (Spatial Ability)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Capacity / Spatial Ability
- **Definition:** Tailor the presentation of visual and verbal information to account for the learner’s ability to mentally manipulate 2D and 3D objects.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Prioritize integrated text and graphics (Spatial Contiguity) and eliminate extraneous material specifically for low-spatial learners. Younger children tend to have low-spatial ability while young adults and adults tend to possess high-spatial ability.
- **Rationale:** High-spatial learners are "resilient"; they can often create their own mental links between separated text and graphics. Low-spatial learners, however, expend so much cognitive energy simply navigating a poorly designed interface that they have no capacity left for actual learning.
- **Boundary Conditions:** As complexity increases, even high-spatial learners reach a "ceiling" and benefit from the supportive design features required by low-spatial learners.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Integrating labels directly next to organs in an anatomy diagram rather than in a separate legend to support learners who struggle with mental rotation.
    - Using "Signaling" (highlighting) to help younger children (low-capacity learners) find the relevant part of an animation quickly.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Presenting a complex mechanical assembly animation to low-spatial learners without any signaling, assuming they can "figure it out" on their own.

### IDD-G3. Adaptive Scaffolding (Dynamic Support)
- **Priority:** Low
- **Category:** Interactivity / Adaptive Learning
- **Definition:** Design instructional systems that sense performance in real-time and adjust the amount of support provided (fading).
- **Rule of Thumb:** Start with high support and gradually "fade" assistance as the learner demonstrates mastery.
- **Rationale:** This aligns with the "Zone of Proximal Development." Keeping the challenge level just above the learner's current ability maintains high engagement and prevents the cognitive overload of too much (or too little) support.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Requires sophisticated back-end logic. In static video or linear documents, this must be applied through **Learner Control** (e.g., allowing the user to choose their own level of detail).
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An AI-driven language app that stops providing translations for words a user has successfully identified multiple times.
    - A branching scenario where an incorrect answer triggers a "hint" animation, while a correct answer advances the user to the next challenge.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A "one-size-fits-all" training video that moves at a medium pace—too slow for experts and too fast for beginners.

# Video Engagement Rules Framework

## Category: Length & Framing

**Master ID Prefix:** VLG

**Core Definition:** Based on the research of Guo, Kim, and Rubin (2014), these rules define the mathematical relationship between video duration, visual style, and student retention. This framework ensures the evaluator bot prioritizes "human attention spans" over "content volume."

## Design Guidelines

### VLG-G1. Optimal Duration Targeting (The 6-Minute Rule)
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Retention)
- **Category:** Video Engagement / Segmenting
- **Definition:** Limiting the total length of a video to align with the median engagement time of online learners.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Target **0–6 minutes** for non-lecture content. For high-stakes lectures, cap at 15 minutes only if high social presence is maintained.
- **Rationale:** Analysis of 6.9 million video sessions shows that median engagement time is at most 6 minutes. Regardless of total length, students rarely watch more than 6–9 minutes, with a **50% drop** in engagement for videos exceeding the 9-minute mark.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This rule is most critical for "first-time" viewers. Re-watchers have a higher dropout rate (78.6%) as they search for specific info; for these users, timestamps and chapters are more vital than total duration.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Breaking a 20-minute software tutorial into four 5-minute "micro-lessons."
    - A 3-minute "concept capsule" explaining a single physics formula (100% completion rate).
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Uploading a continuous 40-minute classroom recording (median watched portion: 20%).
    - Creating 10-minute videos with static slides and voiceover (predicted 60% dropout).

### VLG-G2. Strategic Visual Framing (Medium vs. Long Shot)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Social Agency / Video Production
- **Definition:** Utilizing **Medium Shots** (waist/chest up) for the instructor to maximize social presence and perceived connection.
- **Rule of Thumb:** When the speaker is on camera, use a medium shot or closer. Avoid "Long Shots" (full body/distant) for videos longer than 6 minutes.
- **Rationale:** Medium shots simulate a natural social distance, making the learner feel more connected to the instructor. Research shows Medium Shot + Slides maintains **100% engagement** up to 9 minutes, whereas a Long Shot drops to 65% in the same timeframe.
- **Boundary Conditions:** If the instructor must demonstrate a physical movement (e.g., a lab procedure) that requires seeing the whole body, a Long Shot is required for **Signaling**, overriding the engagement benefit.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Framing an instructor from the chest up during the introduction to create "eye contact."
    - Switching to a medium-shot "talking head" during a 12-minute lecture to boost retention to 75%.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Filming a teacher at the front of a large lecture hall where they appear small and distant.
    - Keeping the instructor in a tiny, distant corner window throughout a 15-minute presentation.

### VLG-G3. Visual Quality Prioritization (Content > Production)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Video Engagement
- **Definition:** Prioritizing the design of visual aids (slides/graphics) over the expensive high-end camera setups for the speaker.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Invest in high-quality slide design and audio narration before investing in "cinema-grade" camera equipment.
- **Rationale:** "Good Slides + Voiceover" consistently outperforms "Speaker + Bad Slides." For 6–9 minute videos, well-designed slides with only a voiceover retain 75% of viewers, whereas a visible speaker paired with poor slides drops to 40%.
- **Boundary Conditions:** This applies to cognitive tasks. If the goal is purely motivational (e.g., a "Welcome" video), the speaker's image takes priority over slide design.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - Using clean, signaled slides with high-quality audio, achieving higher engagement than a low-quality webcam video with bad slides.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Relying on a 4K video of a speaker who is merely reading from cluttered, text-heavy slides.
    - Using "Voiceover + Bad Slides" for a 9-minute video, which statistically results in 75% of the students dropping out.

### VLG-G4. Split-Attention Mitigation (Talking Head Toggle)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Seductive Details
- **Definition:** Removing the speaker's video window when complex visual information (diagrams/animations) is the primary focus.
- **Rule of Thumb:** **Toggle off** the talking head during diagram explanations; bring it back for transitions and summaries.
- **Rationale:** The human face is a high-salience visual cue. When both a face and a complex diagram are on screen, they compete for the limited visual channel, acting as a "seductive detail" that hinders comprehension.
- **Boundary Conditions:** The face should return during "Social Priming" phases (intros/outros) to reset the social relationship.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - The instructor’s video window disappears the moment a complex circuit diagram is displayed.
    - An "Intro/Outro" where the speaker is full-screen, but they are "voice-over only" during the data analysis portion.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Keeping a webcam feed active in the corner while explaining a fast-moving animation of cellular mitosis.
    - A "talking head" that obscures part of the instructional text or diagram on the slide.

### Retention Logic Table (For the Evaluator Bot)

| Video Length | Recommended Style | Expected Retention |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0–6 min | Any style (even bad slides) | 90-100% |
| 6–9 min | Medium Shot Speaker + Good Slides | 100% |
| 6–9 min | Voiceover + Good Slides | 75% |
| 9–12 min | Medium Shot Speaker + Good Slides | 75% |
| 12–40 min | Medium Shot Speaker + Good Slides | 70% |

## Category: Speaking Rate

**Master ID Prefix:** VSR

**Core Definition:** Learners process information more deeply and remain engaged longer when content is delivered at a brisk, concise, and enthusiastic pace. This category measures the "crispness" of delivery as a proxy for instructor preparation and social agency.

## Design Guidelines

### VSR-G1. Optimal Speaking Rate (185–254 wpm)
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Completion Rates)
- **Category:** Video Engagement / Social Agency
- **Definition:** Delivering spoken content at a tempo of 185 to 254 words per minute (wpm).
- **Rule of Thumb:** Target the fastest "optimum" speed where the learner can still process the logic without feeling the need to pause.
- **Rationale:** Research shows a "U-shaped" engagement curve. Mid-range speeds (145–165 wpm) often sound hesitant or low-energy, leading to a 34% drop in completion for 9-minute videos. Brisk rates (185+ wpm) signal high preparation and enthusiasm, which keeps the learner’s "Social Agency" active.
- **Boundary Conditions:** For high-density technical material (e.g., multi-step calculus or complex coding syntax), the rate should drop to the **165–185 wpm** range to allow for essential cognitive processing.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A 10-minute software walkthrough delivered at a "crisp" 210 wpm, keeping the learner's attention locked on the screen.
    - An introductory science video that feels "energetic" and "tight" due to its 220 wpm delivery.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - An instructor speaking at a "leisurely" 130 wpm for a 15-minute video (predicted 80% dropout).
    - Speaking in the "Engagement Dip" zone (145–165 wpm), which often sounds like a disinterested recitation (predicted 80% dropout).

### VSR-G2. Enthusiastic Social Agency (Energy Match)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Social Agency / Personalization Principle
- **Definition:** Pairing a fast speaking rate with high vocal enthusiasm, varied intonation, and purposeful emphasis.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Speed must sound like "teaching," not "reading." If you increase the wpm, you must increase the energy to match.
- **Rationale:** Speed without energy sounds like a "legal disclaimer" at the end of a commercial. Purposeful speed paired with enthusiasm acts as a social cue that motivates the learner to process the material more deeply because the instructor sounds genuinely invested in the topic.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Avoid "manic" or "rushed" delivery. The goal is an energetic persona, not a panicked one.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - An instructor who sounds excited about a specific data point, naturally speeding up to 220 wpm during the "climax" of an explanation.
    - A scripted narration that uses varied pitch and emphasis to maintain a dynamic, "classroom-like" feel despite the fast pace.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - Using a high-speed Text-to-Speech (TTS) voice that is monotone; speed is present, but social agency is absent.

### VSR-G3. Concise Editing & Filler Removal
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Coherence Principle
- **Definition:** Post-production editing to remove "filler" words (um, uh, like) and dead air to increase the **Effective Speaking Rate**.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Edit out any silence longer than 0.5 seconds that does not serve a specific pedagogical purpose (like a "think-break").
- **Rationale:** Filler words are "auditory noise." They lower the effective wpm and force the learner to use cognitive resources to filter out the "garbage" from the "message." Tighter editing prevents the "wandering mind" syndrome. Removing them makes the content more succinct and prevents the disengagement common in students with shorter attention spans.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Do not remove "breath pauses" entirely; the speech should still sound like a natural human, not a "chopped" machine (can be distracting).
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A "jump-cut" style edit that removes an "uhh" as an instructor transitions between two complex slides.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - An unedited 20-minute recording where the instructor frequently pauses for 3 seconds to find their place, dropping the effective wpm to 120.
    - Leaving in "dead air" while a transition occurs on screen, which drops the effective wpm and invites the learner to check their phone.

### VSR-G4. Scripted Pacing Strategy
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Instructional Design / Video Engagement
- **Definition:** Using a detailed script or a tight outline to maintain a fast pace and avoid the "rambling" typical of improvisation.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Script the content to ensure it is non-redundant, then deliver it with the dynamic energy of a face-to-face lecture.
- **Rationale:** Improvisation often leads to "The Rambling Effect"—the instructor takes longer to say less. A script ensures the content is delivered at the "fastest optimum speed" while maintaining logical coherence.
- **Boundary Conditions:** If an instructor is highly experienced and can maintain 185+ wpm while improvising (e.g., "drawing" on a digital whiteboard), a full script may be less necessary than for a standard slide presentation.
- **Aligned Examples:**
    - A well-rehearsed, scripted delivery that allows an instructor to hit 205 wpm without a single stumble.
- **Violating Examples:**
    - A 20-minute video where the instructor "wings it," leading to a slow, 120 wpm pace filled with thinking pauses and off-topic tangents.

# Video Production Style Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** PS

**Core Definition:** A typology of video delivery methods aligned with specific instructional goals. This framework expands the **Multimedia Principle** by prescribing the optimal production format to reduce cognitive load or enhance social agency based on the content type.

While the Multimedia Principles provide the psychological rules, this **Video Production Style Framework** provides the execution strategy.

## Design Guidelines

### PS-G1. Talking Head (Studio-Based)
- **Priority:** Medium (High for Rapport / Low for Technical Data)
- **Category:** Social Agency / Personalization
- **Definition:** Recording the instructor lecturing directly into the camera in a controlled studio setting.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use this format specifically to build emotional rapport, set a tone, or deliver motivational content. Use multiple camera angles (cuts) to maintain visual interest.
- **Rationale:** A visible speaker provides "nurturing value." It triggers the **Social Agency** response, making the learner feel part of a community.
- **Boundary Conditions:** If the instructor’s face remains on screen during complex diagrams, it may act as a **Seductive Detail** (Hansen, 2025).
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - Using a talking head for course introductions or "Why this matters" segments.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - A static talking head that remains visible for 15 minutes of a technical data analysis lecture, potentially acting as a "seductive detail."

### PS-G2. Presentation Slides with Voice-Over
- **Priority:** High (The "Workhorse" Format)
- **Category:** Cognitive Load / Modality Principle
- **Definition:** Full-screen digital slides accompanied by an instructor's audio narration.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Utilize on-slide **Signaling** (annotations/circles) to guide the viewer’s eye in real-time.
- **Rationale:** This format respects the **Image Principle** by toggling off the speaker video so that the learner can focus on instructional contents on the slides.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Slides must adhere to the **Coherence Principle** (no "walls of text") and incorporate enough cognitive/visual scaffolding.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - An instructor circling a specific data outlier in red while explaining its statistical significance.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - Slides that simply show exactly what the narrator is reading word-for-word (**Redundancy Effect**).
    - Slides with overwhelming amounts of text that force the viewer to read rather than listen to the narration.

### PS-G3. Picture-in-Picture (PiP)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Social Agency / Cognitive Load
- **Definition:** Simultaneously displaying the instructor in a small window alongside the primary presentation content.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use PiP only when the social presence of the instructor adds "encouragement" to difficult or dense content.
- **Rationale:** Attempts to combine the "Rapport" of the Talking Head with the "Fidelity" of the Slides.
- **Boundary Conditions:** The instructor window must not obscure instructional graphics and should be "toggled off" during high-complexity visual segments.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - A small instructor window that is visible during bulleted lists but disappears during high-detail animations.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - An instructor window positioned in the corner that blocks the legend or axis labels of a chart.

### PS-G4. Khan-Style Tablet Capture
- **Priority:** High (Critical for STEM/Logic)
- **Category:** Generative Learning / Temporal Contiguity
- **Definition:** Narrated real-time drawing or writing on a digital tablet ("chalk and talk").
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use this for step-by-step problem-solving where the *assembly* of the idea is as important as the final result.
- **Rationale:** Facilitates **Temporal Contiguity** because the explanation and the visual are born simultaneously. It mirrors natural human "teaching motion," which is more engaging than static slide transitions.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Requires legible handwriting and a non-rambling, crisp narration style.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - Drawing a chemical structure bond-by-bond as the narrator explains the valency of the atoms.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - An unscripted session where the instructor spends minutes "scrubbing" errors or writing in illegible cursive.

### PS-G5. Screencast
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Technical/Software training)
- **Category:** Spatial Contiguity / Signaling
- **Definition:** Recording the real-time activity on an instructor’s computer screen (software, websites, code) paired with audio narration.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use clear vocal cues and visual pointers (like cursor highlighting) and zoom into specific UI elements to show the viewer exactly where to direct their attention on the screen and prevent "visual lostness."
- **Rationale:** Versatile and cost-effective for technical trainings. It adheres to the Multimedia Principle by showing the software interface exactly as the learner will experience it, reducing the cognitive load of mental translation.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Can overwhelm if the mouse moves too fast (transient information) or if the screen is too cluttered with irrelevant open windows (Coherence Principle).
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - A coding tutorial where the instructor highlights a specific line of code before executing it.
    - A cybersecurity tutorial demonstrating how to navigate a specific online database with the cursor highlighted in yellow.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - A 10-minute screencast where the instructor's cursor zips across the screen without explanation, leaving the learner behind.
    - A screencast where the instructor performs five rapid-fire steps without pausing or explaining where the cursor is clicking.

### PS-G6. Animation
- **Priority:** Low (High pedagogical value but high resource cost)
- **Category:** Mental Models / Animation Principle
- **Definition:** Using 2D or 3D graphics to visualize abstract concepts, invisible processes (micro-level concepts), or complex relational data.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use animation when the concept is too fast, too slow, or too abstract for real-world video (e.g., molecular motion).
- **Rationale:** Helps learners build **Dynamic Mental Models** of systems that are difficult to mentally animate from static images. Evaluate whether the added pedagogical value (clarity of abstract concepts) justifies the significant time and resource investment.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Must be supported by **Segmenting** (play/pause) to avoid cognitive overload. Avoid "over-animation" where "cool" visuals prioritize aesthetics over instructional clarity (**Coherence Principle**).
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - A simplified 2D animation showing the "Macro vs. Micro" view of how heat causes gas molecules to move.
    - A simple, high-contrast animation explaining the abstract concept of how a MOOC functions.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - A high-budget, "cinematic" animation that uses heavy special effects but lacks a clear instructional "North Star."
    - A high-budget 3D animation that is visually "busy" with particle effects but fails to communicate a clear, singular learning goal.

### PS-G7. Classroom Lecture (Raw Recording)
- **Priority:** Low (The "Least Effective" Format for online-first learning)
- **Category:** Individual Differences / Social Agency / Authenticity
- **Definition:** Filming a traditional in-person lecture as it occurs in a physical classroom.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use this only as a secondary archive; never as the primary instructional medium for online-first courses. If used, take deliberate steps (like dedicated audio or slide-sharing) to ensure online learners do not feel like "secondary" observers.
- **Rationale:** It captures authentic, high-energy educational experience that can build institutional rapport, but it usually fails the Signaling, Image, and Personalization principles for remote viewers.
- **Boundary Conditions:** 
    - Remote students often feel like "secondary" observers, which lowers motivation.
    - Often ineffective for online learning because the pacing and visual signaling are designed for a physical room, not a 6-inch screen.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - A high-energy "Entrepreneurship" guest lecture that includes visible board work and clear student interaction.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - A raw camera feed at the back of a hall where the online student cannot see the blackboard or hear student questions.

### PS-G8. Recorded Seminar
- **Priority:** Low
- **Category:** Social Agency / Community Building
- **Definition:** Recording a group seminar discussion, typically involving an instructor and a small group of students.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Ensure the discussion is structured enough for a remote viewer to follow the logic without feeling like an intruder on an inside joke.
- **Rationale:** Helps online students feel as though they are part of a community of learners, triggering social agency through peer-to-peer modeling.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Requires high-quality multi-mic setups. If the remote viewer cannot hear all participants, the cognitive load of "deciphering" speech prevents learning.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - A "Change the World" seminar where students engage in a focused, visible dialogue with the professor.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - A seminar recording where most participants are inaudible or the camera is fixed on the professor while students speak off-screen.

### PS-G9. Expert Interview
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Social Agency / Expert Insights
- **Definition:** An instructor recording a dialogue with an outside expert to share their professional insights.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Ask targeted, "insider" questions that surface unique commentary students could not find in a textbook.
- **Rationale:** Provides direct access to high-level expertise and diverse perspectives, increasing the "perceived value" and authenticity of the course.
- **Boundary Conditions:** The interview must remain "instructional." If questions are too basic, it adds little value to the core curriculum.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - An architecture pioneer explaining their specific design philosophy during a one-on-one talk with the instructor.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - An interview where the expert simply repeats basic definitions already covered in the module's reading list.

### PS-G10. Informal Conversation
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Social Agency / Personalization Principle
- **Definition:** An informal, typically unscripted discussion about a topic between two instructors or a guest.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Focus on authentic, human reflections to build a personal connection between the presenters and the viewer.
- **Rationale:** Useful for reflecting on course outcomes and providing a dynamic, human perspective that lowers the "power distance" between instructor and student.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Requires a stimulating, focused topic; rambling conversations quickly lose the learner's attention.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - Two instructors sitting informally and reflecting on the most common misconceptions found in the week's discussion board.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - A conversation that rambles off-topic for several minutes, lacking clear instructional relevance.

### PS-G11. Live Synchronous Video
- **Priority:** Medium (High for Support / Low for Scaling)
- **Category:** Social Agency / Real-time Interaction
- **Definition:** Synchronous video events such as virtual office hours, live Q&As, or "Hangouts."
- **Rule of Thumb:** Ensure stable technical connections and schedule events to accommodate multiple time zones.
- **Rationale:** The "gold standard" for establishing instructor presence and providing real-time clarification to complex student questions.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Often becomes a passive "lecture" if not structured for peer-to-peer interaction.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - A live "Global Order" office hour where an instructor answers specific student questions submitted via a real-time chat.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - A live event with poor audio quality where the instructor ignores the student chat to deliver a pre-recorded-style lecture.

### PS-G12. Webcam Capture (Office/Home)
- **Priority:** Medium (High for Agility)
- **Category:** Social Agency / Personalization Principle
- **Definition:** A low-cost, informal version of the Talking Head style recorded outside of a professional studio.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Ensure the background is not distracting and the audio is clear. Use this for "just-in-time" updates.
- **Rationale:** Highly accessible and inexpensive, allowing for rapid content updates that keep a course feeling "fresh" and current.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Must maintain a minimum baseline of professional lighting and resolution to avoid looking "amateurish" to the student.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - A quick "Monday Morning Update" by an instructor providing a summary of the previous week’s grades from their office.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - A weekly update video filmed in a dark room with significant, distracting background noise.

### PS-G13. Physical Demonstration
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Labs/Procedural learning)
- **Category:** Signaling / Procedural Learning
- **Definition:** Filming a physical concept, process, or experiment in action (e.g., a lab experiment or a physical maneuver).
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use close-up shots and multiple angles to ensure every critical step of the process is fully visible.
- **Rationale:** Shows experiments and physical artifacts that students could not otherwise experience remotely, bridging the gap between theory and practice.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Requires careful planning; if the camera angle is too wide, the learner misses the "critical detail" of the demonstration.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - A physics demonstration showing force and motion by having an instructor rappel down a wall with visible measurement tools.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - A chemistry demo where the camera is too far away to see the color change or the texture of the resulting reaction.

### PS-G14. On-Location (Virtual Field Trip)
- **Priority:** Low (High Value for Context)
- **Category:** Social Agency / Contextual Learning
- **Definition:** Recording in a specific, relevant environment outside of a studio (e.g., a construction site, museum, or geological feature).
- **Rule of Thumb:** Have a backup plan for weather and prioritize a "Lavalier" microphone to prevent wind noise.
- **Rationale:** Takes learners to unique places and offers a "virtual field trip" perspective that increases engagement through novelty and realism.
- **Boundary Conditions:** High risk of "auditory noise" (wind/traffic) which can violate the **Voice Principle**.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - A lecture on contemporary architecture filmed with the specific building or skyline as the immediate backdrop.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - An on-location shoot at a beach where wind noise completely drowns out the instructor's narration.

### PS-G15. Green Screen (Chroma Key)
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Social Agency / Spatial Contiguity
- **Definition:** Using a studio setup to substitute digital backgrounds (diagrams/environments) behind a live presenter.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Ensure the substitute background contributes to the learning experience (e.g., a weather-style map) rather than just being decorative.
- **Rationale:** Allows the instructor to be "inside" the content, facilitating **Attention Guidance** through physical pointing at digital elements.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Requires precise "masking" in post-production; poor execution creates a distracting "halo" effect that pulls focus.
- **Aligned Example:** 
    - An English lesson where the instructor appears "in front" of a digital storm to teach weather-related vocabulary using physical gestures.
- **Violating Example:** 
    - Using a green screen to place an instructor in a distracting "tropical island" background for a lecture on financial accounting.

# Visual Scaffolding & Presentation Strategy Framework

**Master ID Prefix:** VSP

**Core Definition:** The strategic use of visual layouts, diagrams, and cues to help learners construct an organized mental model. This framework ensures that information isn't just "shown," but is structured to support the selection, organization, and integration of new knowledge. It provides concrete layouts and tactics to build a mental map for the learner.

It expands the Multimedia Principle and looks into the territory of more specific guidelines for **Visual Scaffolding** to help the learner construct a mental map. Use these guidelines to evaluate whether a video or multimedia material has appropriate and adequate visual scaffolding.

## Design Guidelines

## VSP-I. Data Visualization

### VSP-G1. Numerical Data Mapping
- **Priority:** High (Critical for Cognitive Economy)
- **Category:** Data / Multimedia Principle
- **Definition:** Converting descriptive statistics and raw numerical data into graphical formats like charts or organized tables.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use traditional charts/graphs and **always** label data points with both words and pictures.
- **Rationale:** Humans process visual patterns in data exponentially faster than raw strings of numbers. Visualizing data offloads the cognitive effort of comparison, allowing the brain to focus on the *significance* of the data rather than the calculation.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Avoid "Chart Junk"—3D effects, excessive colors, or distracting textures that do not represent data value (Violates the **Coherence Principle**).
- **Aligned Examples:** 
    - A bar chart comparing market share percentages between three competitors with clear, proximal labels.
    - A line graph showing the rise in academic performance over five years.
- **Violating Examples:** 
    - Listing ten different demographic percentages in a single, dense bulleted list.
    - Using a complex scatter plot for a simple "yes/no" data set.

## VSP-II. Storytelling & Context

### VSP-G2. Case & Scenario Mapping
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Storytelling / Mental Models
- **Definition:** Graphically depicting real or hypothetical cases, situations, contexts, and scenarios using a single image or multiple images, creating comic strips, picture-embedded diagrams, or video clips rather than describing them through written texts.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Show the scene graphically; don't just describe it with written texts. Use single or multiple scene photos or process diagrams with pictures embedded.
- **Rationale:** Storytelling requires the learner to build a pictorial mental model of an environment. Providing the "scene" graphically allows them to focus on the logic of the case (the "why") rather than exhausting working memory trying to imagine/picture the setting (the "where").
- **Boundary Conditions:** Ensure graphics are "Behaviorally Realistic" enough to be credible, but not so detailed they act as **Seductive Details**.
- **Aligned Examples:** 
    - A three-panel comic strip showing a user downloading a file, sharing it illegally, and receiving a legal notice.
    - A short live-action clip of a workplace interaction to demonstrate a HR conflict.
- **Violating Examples:** 
    - A slide with a massive block of text titled "Scenario 1" that the instructor reads aloud (**Redundancy Principle**).

## VSP-III. Procedural, Systemic & Structural

### VSP-G3. Process & Sequence Visualization
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Procedural
- **Definition:** Use graphics (process diagrams, flow charts) or dynamic cues (presenting or highlighting one step at a time) to depict how or in what order tasks or events take place. Simply writing steps and events in the chronological order in a paragraph or using static bullet points without animating them does “not” count as dynamic cues.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use graphics or **dynamic cues** (fade-in animation effect or highlight or spotlight effects) to show the direction of movement.
- **Rationale:** Visualizing a sequence prevents **Temporal Overload**. By seeing the full path, the learner does not have to convert verbal explanations to a visuospatial mental model of how Step A leads to Step B, offloading that task to the video.
- **Boundary Conditions:** For extremely long processes (10+ steps), utilize the **Segmenting Principle**—build the diagram sequentially rather than showing it all at once.
- **Aligned Examples:** 
    - A flowchart showing how an API call travels from a user to a database and back, with the active step highlighted.
- **Violating Examples:** 
    - Listing steps and events using a standard bullet list in the chronological order.
    - Using a standard vertical numbered list to explain a circular or non-linear feedback loop.

### VSP-G4. Systemic Operations & Structures
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Systemic / Spatial Contiguity
- **Definition:** Use graphics and layouts to visuospatially illustrate how parts work together as a whole (Systems) or the physical/conceptual layout of an entity (Structures).
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use relational maps, organizational charts, or "cutaway" blueprints. Prioritize **Abstraction** (clarity) over literal realism.
- **Rationale:** Systems are inherently spatial. If a learner has to mentally "build" the graphic mental model from a verbal description, they exhaust their working memory before they can understand how the system functions.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Strictly adhere to **Spatial Contiguity**. Place labels directly on the components, not in a legend.
- **Aligned Examples:** 
    - A 3D "ghosted" model of a cell's anatomy with labels placed directly on the organelles.
    - A "cutaway" diagram of a mechanical engine showing the internal piston structure.
- **Violating Examples:** 
    - A text list of "Company Departments" without an org chart showing the hierarchical reporting lines.

## VSP-IV. Content Organization

### VSP-G5. Side-by-Side Comparisons
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Organization / Generative Processing
- **Definition:** Placing two or more items in the same visual field to highlight differences or similarities.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use comparison tables or side-by-side layouts. Use both words and icons to distinguish the items.
- **Rationale:** This supports **Generative Processing**. By seeing two items at once, the learner can naturally "integrate" the differences without having to remember what was on a previous slide.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Limit comparisons to 3–4 items to avoid **Split Attention** and visual clutter.
- **Aligned Examples:** 
    - A table comparing the features of the US vs. Japanese educational systems side-by-side.
- **Violating Examples:** 
    - Describing "System A" on slide 5 and "System B" on slide 6, requiring the learner to hold the first in memory.

### VSP-G6. Gridded Lists & Iconic Headings
- **Priority:** Medium
- **Category:** Organization / Signaling
- **Definition:** Organizing text lists into grids and adding icons to headings or items for better scannability whenever appropriate.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Replace standard bullet points with a grid (rows/columns) and add a relevant icon to each block.
- **Rationale:** Icons act as **Signaling** cues. They help the brain categorize information before the text is even read. Grids allow for more dynamic animation (**Segmenting**) of individual points.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Icons must be **Thematically Relevant**. Using "cool" but irrelevant icons violates the **Coherence Principle**.
- **Aligned Examples:** 
    - A 2x2 grid of "Course Goals," each featuring a simple, high-contrast icon (e.g., a pencil icon for 'Writing').
- **Violating Examples:** 
    - A standard slide with 8 bullet points of small, unformatted text.

## VSP-V. No/Low Background Knowledge

### VSP-G7. Unfamiliar & Imperceivable Phenomena
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Scaffolding / Mental Models
- **Definition:** Graphically visualizing objects the learner has never seen or things that cannot be seen with the naked eye (microscopic/macrocosmic).
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use photographs, 3D models or animations. Use **Temporal Zooming** for imperceivable movements (too fast or too slow).
- **Rationale:** Without a "mental image," learners cannot make a good sense of verbal information. Visuals provide the necessary "Prior Knowledge" scaffold for novices.
- **Boundary Conditions:** For conceptual things (like quantum strings), clearly state that the visual is a **Conceptual Model**, not a literal photograph.
- **Aligned Examples:** 
    - A 3D animation of a string vibrating at the quantum level.
    - A high-resolution photo of a new, specialized surgical tool used in a step-by-step tutorial.
- **Violating Examples:** 
    - Describing the "look and feel" of a rare deep-sea fish using only adjectives.

## VSP-VI. Featured Items & Cues

### VSP-G8. Entity & Documentation Archiving
- **Priority:** Low
- **Category:** Social Agency
- **Definition:** Including graphics (pictures, logos, screenshots) of key people, organizations, websites, or articles mentioned.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use Logos, Screenshots, or Headshots as "Signposts."
- **Rationale:** These serve as **Social Agency** cues. Seeing the actual journal cover or the founder's face makes the information feel "real" and grounded in reality.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Keep these visuals small and secondary; they should not distract from the primary instructional goal.
- **Aligned Examples:** 
    - Show a Walmart logo when you talk about Walmart.
    - Placing a small logo of a research lab next to a citation of their study.
    - A screenshot of a website's landing page when discussing its UI design.
- **Violating Examples:** 
    - Mentioning "The New York Times" but using a generic, unrelated clip-art image of a newspaper.
    - Mentioning “The New York Times” but showing a QR code of the newspaper website.

### VSP-G9. Attention Direction (Visual Cues)
- **Priority:** High
- **Category:** Signaling / Attention Guidance
- **Definition:** Using graphic overlays (arrows, boxes, spotlights) to emphasize specific parts of the screen.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Use spotlight effects or magnifiers and pair them with animations (appearing/fading).
- **Rationale:** This is the **Signaling Principle** in action. Cues tell the learner: "Ignore the noise; focus right here." It prevents the "Visual Search" that exhausts cognitive resources.
- **Boundary Conditions:** Use sparingly. **If everything is highlighted, nothing is highlighted.**
- **Aligned Examples:** 
    - A "spotlight" effect that dims the rest of the screen except for the specific active button in a software tutorial.
- **Violating Examples:** 
    - Narrating a specific data point on a complex, colorful chart without any visual indication of its location.
